Yuval Noah Harari, known for his expansive views on human history and civilization, might argue that economists are indeed storytellers. In his perspective, storytelling is a fundamental human tool that enables large-scale cooperation by creating shared myths and narratives. Economists, then, fit into this framework by crafting narratives to explain and make sense of complex economic systems. These aren’t just fanciful tales—they’re stories grounded in data and models, designed to predict economic trends and influence human behavior.

However, Harari would likely add a layer of caution. He often emphasizes the limitations of human constructs, pointing out that our stories—however useful—aren’t absolute truths. Economic narratives, with all their charts and equations, are still products of human imagination and interpretation. They can be incomplete or flawed, and Harari might warn against the hubris of treating them as infallible. This aligns with his broader critique of overconfidence in the systems we build to understand the world.

Moreover, Harari might highlight the power these economic stories wield. Because they shape policies, guide markets, and affect how societies function, they don’t just describe reality—they actively mold it. This dual role as both explanation and influence underscores the responsibility economists bear. Harari would likely urge them to approach their craft with humility, recognizing the limits of their narratives while striving for accuracy and clarity.

In short, Harari might say that economists are storytellers whose tales, built on data and theory, help us navigate economic complexity. Yet, he’d remind us that these stories are human-made, potent in their ability to shape reality, and thus demand careful construction and constant scrutiny.
