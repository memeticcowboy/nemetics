The Coen Brothers use The Stranger to blur the lines between the film’s fictional world and the audience’s perspective, creating a layer of self-awareness that enriches the narrative.

The Stranger appears at the beginning and end of the film, framing the story of Jeffrey "The Dude" Lebowski with a folksy, omniscient voiceover. Right from his opening monologue, he introduces the Dude in a way that feels both mythic and tongue-in-cheek: "Way out west there was this fella... fella I wanna tell ya about." His tone is deliberately anachronistic, evoking a cowboy storyteller from a Western, which contrasts with the modern, absurd Los Angeles setting of the film. This dissonance hints at the meta-reference—he’s not just narrating, he’s winking at the audience about the artificiality of the tale.

What makes The Stranger a meta device is how he occasionally steps beyond a passive narrator role. For instance, when he says, "Sometimes there’s a man—I won’t say a hero, ’cause what’s a hero?—but sometimes there’s a man," he’s not only describing the Dude but also questioning the very conventions of storytelling. He’s aware he’s spinning a yarn, and his hesitations and asides ("I lost m’train of thought here") suggest he’s improvising or reflecting on the narrative as it unfolds, much like a filmmaker or writer might. This self-consciousness pulls the audience out of immersion just enough to recognize the constructed nature of the film.

The meta-reference deepens when The Stranger interacts with the Dude directly at the bowling alley bar near the end. Up until this point, he’s been an external voice, but here he becomes part of the story, ordering a sarsaparilla and chatting with the Dude about the events we’ve just witnessed. This crossing of narrative boundaries—where the narrator enters the diegetic world—blurs the line between observer and participant. It’s as if The Stranger is stepping out of his role to check in on the character he’s been narrating, reinforcing the idea that the film is a self-aware construct. His final line to the audience, "The Dude abides, and I don’t know about you, but I take comfort in that," directly addresses us, sealing the meta-connection by acknowledging our presence as viewers.

Through The Stranger, the Coen Brothers playfully comment on the act of storytelling itself—its arbitrariness, its charm, and its reliance on larger-than-life figures like the Dude. He’s not just a narrator; he’s a meta-reference to the absurdity of trying to impose order or meaning on a chaotic, shaggy-dog story, mirroring the film’s own laid-back philosophy.
