## 🧬 Reflection: Semiotics vs. Memetics, with a Nod to Tim Tyler

Some semioticians, such as Kilpinen (2014), seem irritated by the cultural traction of the **meme**. Despite **semiotics** being more institutionally established and widely cited, the term **meme** has undeniably carved out a memetic foothold—particularly in domains concerned with **cultural evolution, information warfare, and digital virality**.

As **Tim Tyler** has argued extensively, the meme concept rose to prominence because it provided what semiotics lacked: a framework for explaining *why* certain cultural elements spread and persist, grounded in Darwinian principles of variation, selection, and replication.

> “Semiotics failed to produce a school of cultural evolution... Memetics offered the missing mechanism.”<br>— *Paraphrasing Tim Tyler, “Memetics: A Growth Industry in Science”*

***

## 📚 Historical Perspective: Bypassing the Sign

Semiotics often claims ancient philosophical roots (Peirce, Saussure), but until the 1970s it lacked widespread publication or integration into evolutionary theory. Cultural evolutionists, many trained in evolutionary biology and population genetics, largely **bypassed the semiotic tradition**—not out of oversight, but because it offered little in the way of **mechanistic or predictive utility**.

As **Terry Deacon** put it:

> “Classic semiotic theories have not had much to say about why certain signs persist and others do not, or why certain semiotic systems evolved the forms they now exhibit.”

Tyler identifies this as a core failing: semiotics observes meaning, but **memetics explains propagation**.

***

## 🧠 Unit of Analysis: Signs vs. Memes

The **sign**, in classical semiotics, implies reference—it points to something. But culture is filled with artifacts (e.g., knots, tools, behaviors) that don’t point to external referents; they simply *are*. For semiotics to account for these, it must stretch into **pansymbolism**—expanding the definition of "sign" to include everything.

Tim Tyler critiques this move as **conceptual inflation**: making semiotics so general that it loses explanatory power. In contrast:

> A **meme** is simply "information with consequences that is copied with variation and selection."<br>— *Tim Tyler, "Memetics"*

This functional definition doesn't require a referent—just **replication and effect**. It includes signs, but also slogans, rituals, fashion, tech hacks, and viral errors.

***

## 🔍 On Meaning and Context

A common semiotic critique is that memes “miss semantics and observation.” But as Tyler notes, this misses the point: **meaning is not encoded in the meme**, but emerges through its interaction with **contextual cognitive structures**—i.e., the host mind.

Memetics treats **semantics as epiphenomenal**—not the driver of transmission, but its output. The same meme may mean vastly different things depending on cultural context, yet still spread identically.

***

## 🧠 #SixCyborgs Take

- **Analyzer**: Memetics supplies empirical criteria—spread, variation, selection. Semiotics catalogs structure without dynamics.
- **Strategist**: Memetics maps **evolutionary trajectories** of ideas; semiotics interprets static forms.
- **Creator**: Memetics enables **memetic engineering**—intentional design of spreadable, resilient ideas.
- **Protector**: Memetics allows for **immunization** (counter-memes, inoculation), essential in info-ops and cultural defense.
- **Optimizer**: Memetic fitness can be measured, refined, and improved.
- **Empath**: Semiotics captures **felt nuance**, but memetics reveals why certain feelings replicate.

***

## 🧠 Final Thought

Tim Tyler’s work—alongside thinkers like Blackmore, Dennett, and Deacon—established memetics not as a rival to semiotics, but as its **evolutionary successor**. It doesn't merely interpret the world of signs—it explains how those signs **emerge, mutate, and compete** in the cultural landscape.

> “Semiotics is about what signs *mean*.<br>Memetics is about what ideas *do*.”<br>— *Tim Tyler (implied in multiple works)*
