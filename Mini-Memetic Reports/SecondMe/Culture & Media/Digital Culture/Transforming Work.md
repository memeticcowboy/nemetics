# Transforming Work Environments Through Gaming: Insights from Total Engagement

Created at 2025/01/25 9:53 AM

TOTAL ENGAGEMENT

Using Games and Virtual Worlds to Change the Way People Work and Businesses

Compete

Byron Reeves and Jay Leighton Read

Harvard Business Press, 2009

By Jan Wyllie ( j.wyllie@open-intelligence.co.uk )

I must confess that despite all the claims on the covers is extremely rare for a

management book to engage me in a real game changing experience.

It isn’t that I hadn’t been primed. At the beginning of this year, the RSA put on a

lecture by Tom Chatfield, with the title of Fun Inc.: Why games are the 21st

century’s most serious business. I didn’t go, but I listened to the podcast. Since then,

I noticed that the ‘gaming’ stream in our Social Media Monitor was changing in

character. Gaming was no longer just about a successful fast growing industry aimed

at a youth culture of what I saw as alienation and escapism. Gaming was growing up,

and, yes, it is becoming serious, and ironically as a way of actually combating

alienation and escapism to boot.

So when our gaming intelligence agent on Amplify, Simon Bostock,

( http://bfchirpy.amplify.com/ ) alerted me to TOTAL ENGAGEMENT, I decided to

make an exception to my policy of not reading management books. I am very glad

that I did!

Unlike most management books which take one often blindingly obvious idea and

repeat it chapter after chapter, interspersed with personal stories of the authors, this

book is so packed with useful content that it is hard to summarise.

It takes the reader from a rather hazy and typically biased understanding of gaming to

beginning to think about how to turn their work environment into a form of

multiplayer game, not just to make working (living!) more fun, but also to make

collaboration more meaningful and productive. Indeed, we are, as I write, designing a

social intelligence monitoring game rewarding “intelligence scoops” with Reeves &amp;

Read call “synthetic currencies”, a.k.a. community currencies, complementary

currencies and social currencies in the real world.

Massive multiplayer online role-playing games (MMOs, for short), such as World of

Warcraft (WoW) have for years now enabled teams of players, known as Guilds, to

collaborate to accomplish complex, highly knowledge intensive tasks in order to make

raids on other teams and to vanquish artificial monsters generated by the game,

known, slightly worryingly for bosses, as “bosses”. One of many great take-away

thoughts of the book is that in MMO gaming environments, leadership is not a

position, as in Chief Executive Officer or Prime Minister, but is a role that can change

from person to person during the course of a game depending on who has the relevant

skills.

The nature of games strongly motivates players to improve their performance, which

also means improving their skills, ideal for RL (real life) business and living. It

requires Guild members to acquire the knowledge and coordination to avoid “wipe

out” and to enjoy “epic wins”. Psychological studies are cited by the authors

indicating that the relationships between players, through their Avatars, feel as real, as

relationships in RL.

Game worlds supply players with real time, sophisticated performance feedback

metrics that would be the envy of any personnel manager. MMOs generate self-

signifying statistics, too. (See IK, KM Frontiers: Self signifying knowledge, Sep

2009). The authors emphasise the success of MMOs in creating “a thoughtful context

where people have tools for focusing and filtering” as a way of dealing with

information overload.

The authors caution against some of the known risks of gaming, such as fostering

aggression, addiction and stress, rather than engagement. They make the point that in

business applications ethical questions are raised because real world gamers cannot

just leave they game if they do not like it, since, unlike WoW players, their living

depends on it.

It must be said that the personal stories of business gaming at the beginning of each

chapter, although both plausible and compelling, are all imaginary. Business is not

actually “gamifying”… in these hard times, quite the opposite. But as economic

straights make work increasingly pressured at the same time as being less rewarding

financially, perhaps, as the authors suggest, the time is coming for business and

government to ‘lighten up’ using the motivation and reward systems so successfully

applied by gamers.

The place to start is TOTAL ENGAGEMENT. Don’t be put off by the dryness of the

first two Chapters, nor the drab cover. Work is not meant to be fun … yet.
