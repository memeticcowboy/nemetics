## 2. Conditional/Targeted Pause Advocates

### Threshold-Based Approaches

A more pragmatic middle ground has emerged around conditional pause proposals that would trigger based on demonstrated capabilities or risk assessments rather than blanket prohibitions.

**Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)**, led by CEO Dario Amodei and Responsible Scaling Officer Jared Kaplan, represents the most developed industry framework for conditional pauses. The RSP establishes "AI Safety Levels" (ASL) modeled on biosafety levels, with increasingly stringent requirements as capabilities grow. Key elements include:[^23][^24][^25][^26]

- **Capability Thresholds**: Defined danger levels (ASL-3, ASL-4) that would trigger enhanced safeguards or development pauses

- **Pre-deployment Testing**: Models must be evaluated to determine if they are "unlikely to reach relevant Capability Thresholds" before training proceeds

- **Conditional Deployment**: "If a model demonstrates high degrees of capability on any risk vector...we will stop training until suitable mitigations can be developed"[^27]

However, critics note that Anthropic's October 2024 RSP update significantly weakened these commitments by removing predefined evaluation thresholds in favor of CEO judgment on capability reports. The policy now states that Amodei and Kaplan will "make the ultimate determination as to whether we have sufficiently established that we are unlikely to reach the Capability Threshold".[^24]

**Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework** establishes "Critical Capability Levels" (CCLs) with similar logic: "A model may reach evaluation thresholds before mitigations at appropriate levels are ready. If this happens, we would put on hold further deployment or development". However, the framework's March 2025 release of Gemini 2.5 Pro without timely safety documentation drew criticism from 60 UK parliamentarians for violating international safety commitments.[^28][^29][^30]

### If-Then Commitments

The concept of "if-then commitments" has gained traction as a politically viable compromise. Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, and other leading researchers endorsed this framework in their 2023 paper "Managing extreme AI risks amid rapid progress." The structure creates conditional obligations:[^21][^30]

**IF** dangerous capabilities are demonstrated (e.g., ability to substantially assist in creating biological weapons)

**AND** alignment/safety solutions remain unproven

**THEN** development must pause until adequate safeguards exist

This approach attempts to bridge disagreements about AI timelines and risk imminence by deferring action until concrete danger thresholds are crossed. The Existential Risk Observatory's Conditional AI Safety Treaty operationalizes this through AI Safety Institutes (AISIs) that would conduct independent evaluations and have authority to mandate pauses.[^31][^22][^21]

### Sector-Specific and Capability-Specific Pauses

Several voices advocate for pauses targeting specific high-risk applications rather than all AI development:

**Stuart Russell**, UC Berkeley computer scientist and co-author of the standard AI textbook, focuses on preventing development of autonomous systems that cannot be controlled or whose objectives might misalign with human values. Russell advocates for fundamental research into "provably beneficial" AI architectures before deploying systems with autonomous goal-pursuit capabilities.[^32][^33]

**Connor Leahy**, CEO of AI safety company Conjecture, has called for immediate regulation of specific high-risk outputs like deepfakes as a "first step" toward broader governance. In his 2024 TIME interview, Leahy argued: "This is absolutely a thing that is doable, and would imminently buy us all more time to actually build the long term technical and socio-political solutions". However, Leahy remains firmly in the hard pause camp regarding artificial general intelligence, arguing that racing toward AGI "is incredibly dangerous" and could require a pause lasting decades.[^34][^35][^36][^37]
