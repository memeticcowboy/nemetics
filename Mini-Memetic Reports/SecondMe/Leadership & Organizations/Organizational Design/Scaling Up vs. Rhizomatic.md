# Scaling Up vs. Rhizomatic Seeding: Approaches to Systemic Change

Created at 2025/06/11 9:25 PM

In movements for systemic change, a frequent question emerges: how do we spread what works without compromising the integrity of the work itself?

Two common answers show up in practice: scaling up and what I’ll call rhizomatic seeding.

Though both seek to extend impact across geographical or social boundaries, their logic, structure, and consequences couldn’t be more different.

“Scaling up” borrows its language from business and tech. It presumes that a successful model should be replicated at a larger size, often by standardizing its core components and establishing central oversight or franchise, like models of deployment.

In this model, there’s often a blueprint or toolkit that defines how the project should be done elsewhere.

The original node (project, org, or initiative) remains privileged as the source of legitimacy or correctness.

Replication tends to be linear and hierarchical; someone teaches, others learn.

Evaluation is based on scaling metrics: how many new sites, how many users, how much growth.

It’s tempting because it gives a sense of control. But it carries a shadow: local contexts become subordinate to the model, and innovation gets replaced by compliance.

Rhizomatic Seeding is inspired by the biological structure of rhizomes, underground root systems that sprout laterally in unpredictable ways. It refers to a horizontal, adaptive, and non-centralized approach to community replication.

In this model, projects are seeded, not cloned. What’s shared is an invitation, not an instruction manual.

New nodes emerge autonomously, adapting to local soil, culture, and needs.

Legitimacy is distributed, not retained by a single origin point.

The process is non-linear, often slow, often invisible, until it suddenly isn't. 

Here, the goal is not the same project in more places, but more projects of the same spirit. The strength lies in diversity, not uniformity.

Rhizomatic seeding is harder to quantify. It resists easy narratives of growth. But it offers something precious: resilience through plurality.

It asks different questions:

Not: “How do we replicate this model everywhere?”

But: “How do we spread the conditions for many unique forms to emerge?”

It shifts the emphasis from institutional fidelity to ecosystem vitality.

If scaling up imagines a pyramid or tower (more levels, more reach) rhizomatic seeding imagines a mycelial network, always growing underground, always capable of sprouting unexpected fruit. 

In the work of community building, justice, or liberation, the question isn’t just how big we can grow something.

It’s how deeply it can root, and how widely it can resonate, without ever becoming a cage.

Rhizomatic seeding honors the wisdom that freedom doesn’t come from the center, and that real power spreads sideways.

Let’s stop scaling up and start seeding across.
