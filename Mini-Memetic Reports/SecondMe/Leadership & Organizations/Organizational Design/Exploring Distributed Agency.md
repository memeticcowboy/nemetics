# 5. 🔗 Distributed and Relational Agency

Created at 2025/06/12 11:31 AM

### Key Points

- Research suggests agency is distributed across humans, machines, and environments, not just individuals, as seen in Actor-Network Theory, Extended Mind, and Enactivism.
- It seems likely that responsibility and morality emerge from these socio-technical systems, with controversy over how to balance individual and collective accountability.
- The evidence leans toward sharing accountability in fields like autonomous weapons, algorithmic governance, and deepfake media, but assigning it remains complex and debated.

#### Distributed and Relational Agency

Agency isn’t just about what one person does; it’s spread out across networks of people, machines, institutions, and places. Think of it like a team effort where everyone and everything plays a part. For example, when you use a GPS to navigate, it’s not just you deciding the route—the GPS, your phone, and even the road signs are all involved.

- **Actor-Network Theory (ANT)**: This idea, from Bruno Latour, says things like tools or buildings can have agency too, not just people. So, in a hospital, the MRI machine helps diagnose, sharing the agency with doctors.
- **Extended Mind**: Andy Clark and David Chalmers say your mind extends to things like notebooks or smartphones. If you write a to-do list, that list is part of how you think and act.
- **Enactivism**: This suggests your actions and thoughts come from interacting with the world, like how you learn by doing, not just thinking.

#### Implication for Responsibility and Morality

If agency is shared, then who’s to blame when something goes wrong? It’s not just one person; it’s the whole system. For instance, if an autonomous weapon makes a bad call, it’s not just the soldier’s fault—it’s also the designers, the company that made it, and the rules set by leaders. The same goes for algorithms deciding who gets a loan or deepfakes spreading lies; it’s a team effort, and morality comes from how the system works together.

#### Challenge of Assigning Accountability

Figuring out who’s responsible in these big networks is tough. For autonomous weapons, it might mean sharing blame among makers, users, and policymakers. In algorithmic governance, like deciding welfare, companies and regulators share the load. For deepfakes, it could be creators, platforms, and even viewers. Solutions might include new laws, making systems transparent, or educating people, but it’s still a big debate, especially with sensitive issues like weapons or misinformation.
