# Navigating Authority in Human–AI Interaction Guidelines

Created at 2026/01/15 8:20 AM

What the CHI Human–AI Guidelines Are Actually Doing<br><br>If you strip away the UX language, the SIGCHI Human–AI Interaction guidelines aren’t trying to define what AI is. They’re trying to keep AI systems from quietly hardening into authority.<br><br>They start from an unspoken assumption:<br>AI systems are already unpredictable, adaptive, and a little opaque. You can’t make them perfectly right. What you can do is stop them from freezing into something people feel they must obey.<br><br>The real danger isn’t that AI makes mistakes.<br>It’s that people stop noticing when it could be mistaken.<br><br>The guidelines act like shock absorbers. Not ethics. Not doctrine. Just damping—so coordination doesn’t snap into control.<br><br>⸻<br><br>1. Clarity as “Don’t Let the Interface Pretend to Be Reality”<br><br>When the guidelines say things like:<br>	•	make clear what the system can do<br>	•	show how well it does it<br>	•	help users discover limits<br><br>they’re not chasing usability polish. They’re protecting the moment where a person first notices something.<br><br>If an interface is vague or overconfident, people don’t just misunderstand it—they hand over judgment. The tool starts to feel like the ground itself.<br><br>CHI is quietly saying:<br>Don’t let the interface cosplay as truth.<br><br>Labels are meant to stay labels. If they harden into belief, the system starts shaping reality instead of participating in it.<br><br>⸻<br><br>2. Timing Rules Are About Emotional Drift, Not Speed<br><br>A lot of the guidelines talk about timing:<br>	•	adapting gradually<br>	•	responding in context<br>	•	learning cautiously<br>	•	encouraging feedback<br><br>This isn’t about cognition. It’s about how things feel over time.<br><br>When systems change too fast, or too smoothly, people fall into loops:<br>	•	surprise feels like betrayal<br>	•	assistance feels like expectation<br>	•	convenience feels like obligation<br><br>You start hearing sentences like:<br><br>“It always does this.”<br>“It expects me to act this way.”<br>“I feel behind and I don’t know why.”<br><br>CHI is trying to prevent the system from setting the emotional tempo of someone’s life. Coordination has to remain optional. The tool should never feel like it’s keeping score on your behalf.<br><br>⸻<br><br>3. Memory, Explanations, and Controls Are About Preventing Scars<br><br>When the guidelines insist on:<br>	•	explaining behavior<br>	•	notifying changes<br>	•	allowing pause, reset, undo<br><br>they’re managing how tightly patterns lock in.<br><br>AI systems form habits quickly—often faster than users can make sense of them. If memory accumulates without explanation, people feel tracked. If explanations come without control, they feel theatrical. If control exists without memory, everything feels random.<br><br>The pause and reset buttons matter most. They’re not convenience features. They’re escape hatches.<br><br>They let someone say:<br><br>“Stop building a world around me.”<br><br>That’s not interface design. That’s keeping coordination from becoming destiny.<br><br>⸻<br><br>4. Error Handling Is About Keeping Alternatives Alive<br><br>CHI treats error as normal, fixable, and non-moral.<br><br>That sounds obvious—until you notice how many systems do the opposite:<br>	•	errors increase friction<br>	•	correction feels like confession<br>	•	deviation gets expensive<br><br>In those systems, mistakes don’t teach—they punish. And punishment is how control hardens.<br><br>By making recovery fast and reversible, the guidelines keep multiple paths open. Nothing has to be justified. Nothing has to be defended. You can try again without paying a toll.<br><br>That’s how you stop systems from turning “wrong” into “forbidden.”<br><br>⸻<br><br>5. Why There Is No Single “Official” AI Heuristic<br><br>SIGCHI never issued one sacred list of rules. That’s not indecision—it’s restraint.<br><br>A single canonical heuristic would:<br>	•	flatten context<br>	•	turn coordination into doctrine<br>	•	accelerate lock-in across fields<br><br>Instead, CHI keeps families of guidelines, evolving workshops, and local adaptations. Enough coherence to move forward. Not enough closure to dominate.<br><br>That’s what a healthy coordination culture looks like:<br>shared orientation without claiming final authority.<br><br>⸻<br><br>The Big Picture<br><br>Read this way, the CHI guidelines aren’t about making AI “nice” or “ethical.”<br><br>They’re about slowing the moment when tools start feeling inevitable.<br><br>They manage:<br>	•	how things are noticed<br>	•	how they accumulate emotionally<br>	•	how habits form<br>	•	how recovery works<br>	•	how much authority the system is allowed to claim<br><br>They don’t prevent collapse.<br>But they buy time. They keep reversibility alive.<br><br>⸻<br><br>One-Line Diagnosis (Plain Language)<br><br>The SIGCHI Human–AI Interaction guidelines are an attempt to keep AI systems useful without letting them quietly harden into unquestioned reality—by managing clarity, timing, memory, and recovery so coordination never becomes truth.<br><br>That they emerged from practice instead of philosophy is exactly why they work.
