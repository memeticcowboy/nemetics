## 3. Enhanced Guardrails \& Governance Advocates

### Mandatory Safety Testing and Regulatory Frameworks

A substantial coalition advocates for robust regulatory oversight without necessarily calling for development pauses, though many acknowledge pauses may become necessary if safety cannot be demonstrated.

**Center for AI Safety (CAIS)**, founded and directed by Dan Hendrycks, occupies a pivotal position bridging technical safety research and policy advocacy. CAIS organized the May 2023 statement signed by over 500 AI researchers and industry leaders (including Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, and Dario Amodei) declaring: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war". Hendrycks serves as AI Safety Advisor to xAI while maintaining CAIS's focus on societal-scale risks. The organization provides compute clusters for safety research, runs educational programs, and actively lobbied for California's SB 1047.[^38][^39][^40][^41][^42][^43][^44]

**California SB 1047**: This vetoed 2024 legislation represented the most significant US state-level attempt to mandate AI safety practices. The bill would have required:[^45][^46][^47][^48]

- Safety and security protocols for "covered models" (those trained with >\$100M in compute or fine-tuned for >\$10M)

- Pre-deployment testing for catastrophic risks (mass casualties, >\$500M infrastructure damage)

- "Kill switch" shutdown capabilities

- Liability for negligent safety practices

The bill drew support from Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, over 100 current/former employees of major AI labs, and (after amendments) Anthropic. Opposition came from major AI companies, prominent AI researchers including Yann LeCun and Fei-Fei Li, venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz, and California congressional members including Nancy Pelosi. Governor Gavin Newsom's veto cited concerns that the bill's blanket approach based on compute thresholds failed to account for actual deployment contexts and risks.[^47][^48][^49][^45]

### Industry Self-Regulation vs. Government Intervention

The governance debate reveals deep fissures between those trusting voluntary industry commitments and those demanding legally enforceable requirements.

**Voluntary Commitments**: The 2023 White House AI commitments and 2024 Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments represent the voluntary approach, with companies pledging to conduct safety testing and provide transparency. However, enforcement mechanisms remain absent, and companies have been accused of violating these commitments. The March 2025 Gemini 2.5 Pro release controversy exemplifies these tensions—Google released the model publicly, then added safety documentation over a month later, despite commitments to share models with government AI safety institutes before deployment.[^30][^28][^45]

**Government Regulation Advocates**: Max Tegmark's Senate testimony argued that voluntary commitments are insufficient: "Innovation depends on regulation and oversight...The same can and should be done for AI". Tegmark proposed:[^5]

- Mandatory licensing for highly-capable general-purpose AI systems

- Independent audits before deployment

- Burden of proof on developers to demonstrate safety

- Refusal to license "uncontrollable AI" systems until adequate protocols exist[^5]

Emily Bender (University of Washington linguistics professor) and Timnit Gebru (founder of Distributed AI Research Institute) represent critical voices emphasizing that AI systems cannot be divorced from the corporate power structures producing them. Gebru, fired from Google in 2020 after co-authoring the "Stochastic Parrots" paper warning about large language model risks, advocates for transparency, accountability, and democratization of AI development rather than accepting current corporate-dominated paradigms.[^50][^51][^52][^53][^54]

### Emerging International Frameworks

**EU AI Act**: The world's first comprehensive AI regulation entered force August 2024, implementing a risk-based approach:[^55][^56][^57]

- Unacceptable risk systems (social scoring, manipulative AI) are banned

- High-risk systems face strict obligations (risk assessment, human oversight, documentation)

- General-purpose AI models with systemic risk must conduct evaluations and adversarial testing

- Transparency requirements for limited-risk systems (chatbots, deepfakes must be labeled)

**UN Global Digital Compact**: Adopted at the September 2024 UN General Assembly, the Compact commits to five action areas:[^58][^59]

- Closing the digital divide in AI access and capability

- Ensuring AI systems align with human rights and democratic values

- Data governance for human benefit

- Establishing international AI governance mechanisms

- Creating an international scientific panel on AI

**Council of Europe Framework Convention**: The first legally binding international AI treaty, signed by 46 member states and 36 others in 2024, establishes requirements for risk assessment, transparency, accountability, and human rights protections.[^60][^61]

These frameworks generally avoid explicit pause provisions but establish governance infrastructure that could enable conditional pauses if risks materialize.
