### Manipulation Mechanisms and Framing

Organizations often use subtle tactics to influence employees, such as reframing resistance to change as "feedback" to make it seem constructive, or co-opting dissent by treating it as "data" for improvement. This can mask influence operations, using terms like "streamlining" for layoffs to soften the impact. These strategies help align employees with organizational goals but may hide true intentions.

### Censorship-by-Omission in Change Management

Typical change management often focuses on efficiency and strategy, potentially overlooking labor power (employees' influence), structural inequality (disparities in impact across levels), and consent boundaries (employee voice in decisions). To address these, organizations can engage employees early, assess impacts on different groups, and use inclusive communication to surface deeper issues.

### Heroic Language vs. Inclusive Approach

Heroic language, such as "burning rulebooks" or "trailblazing dashboards," can glamorize top-down change, framing leaders as heroes guiding the organization through challenges. However, an inclusive approach questions this, emphasizing empathy, collaboration, and meeting employees where they are, fostering cultural change through relatable, supportive language rather than hierarchical narratives.

#### Manipulation Mechanisms and Linguistic Tactics

Manipulation in organizations involves subtle strategies to influence employee behavior, often through language that reframes or co-opts dissent. Research highlights that organizations frequently rebrand resistance to change as "feedback," making it appear constructive rather than obstructive. For instance, change management models, such as those by Kotter and Schlesinger, include co-option as a tactic where resistant individuals are brought into the change process, potentially neutralizing their opposition by giving them symbolic roles. This is evident in discussions on overcoming resistance, where co-option involves integrating managers likely to resist into planning groups, possibly for appearances rather than substantive contribution.

Similarly, dissent is often co-opted as "data," suggesting it is being used to improve processes rather than challenging authority. This linguistic framing helps maintain organizational control while presenting a facade of inclusivity. Euphemisms and evocative terms further mask influence operations; for example, "streamlining" might be used for layoffs, or "optimization" for cost-cutting measures that disproportionately affect lower-level employees. These tactics align with broader observations in organizational behavior literature, where language is used to shape perceptions and reduce friction during change initiatives.

#### Censorship-by-Omission: Overlooked Aspects in Change Management

Censorship-by-omission occurs when change management practices fail to address critical human and social dimensions, such as labor power, structural inequality, and consent boundaries. Typical change management models often prioritize technical and procedural aspects, such as project timelines and system implementations, over the emotional and political realities of change. Research suggests that labor power—employees' ability to influence or resist change—is frequently underestimated, with initiatives assuming compliance without genuine engagement. Structural inequality, where changes exacerbate disparities (e.g., cost-cutting affecting lower levels more than management), is another overlooked area, as models may not assess differential impacts across organizational hierarchies.

Consent boundaries, or the extent to which employees have a meaningful voice in decisions, are also often neglected. Critical perspectives, such as those in Mark Hughes' work, argue that rationalist and teleological approaches are better suited to technical processes than to complex emotions and politics, labeling change issues as "wicked" (Rittel and Webber, 1973)—lacking straightforward solutions. To surface these deeper issues, organizations must adopt pluralist approaches, engaging employees early, conducting thorough impact assessments, and using inclusive communication strategies that acknowledge concerns about power, equity, and consent.

#### Heroic Language vs. Inclusive Approaches in Cultural Change

Language plays a pivotal role in shaping perceptions of change, with heroic narratives often glamorizing top-down initiatives. Terms like "burning rulebooks" or "trailblazing dashboards" evoke dramatic, transformative actions, positioning leaders as visionaries or heroes guiding the organization through challenges. This is exemplified by the hero's journey narrative, popularized by Joseph Campbell (1949), which structures change as a journey from the "known world" (current state) to the "call to adventure" (need for change), through trials and tests, to the "return with the elixir" (successful change). Such language can inspire but risks alienating employees, reinforcing hierarchical dynamics.

In contrast, an inclusive approach questions these old language tactics, emphasizing empathy, collaboration, and psychological safety. This involves meeting employees where they are, understanding their concerns, and addressing them honestly, rather than relying on grandiose, top-down narratives. Research suggests that inclusive communication fosters cultural change by encouraging storytelling and dialogue, allowing employees to share experiences and feel heard. For instance, modern change management practices advocate for fireside chats or storytelling around shared goals, moving away from hierarchical language to create a more relatable and supportive environment. This approach aligns with empathetic leadership, ensuring change is not just a technical process but a deeply human one, addressing the psychological needs of employees.
