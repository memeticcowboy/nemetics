**Heuristic**: Streisand Effect Awareness

**Description:** Know that attempting to suppress or hide information can backfire and draw more attention to it. Named after Barbra Streisand’s futile attempt to censor photos of her house (which then went viral), this heuristic warns that sometimes the cover-up is worse than the crime. Transparency or a low-key response may do less reputational damage than aggressive secrecy. 

**Use Case:** A company facing a minor scandal might be tempted to threaten journalists or quietly delete negative reviews. The heuristic suggests this could blow up – instead, openly acknowledge the issue and address it. Tech firms often decide not to sue trivial trademark infringements because the lawsuit would generate more negative PR than the infringement itself. Essentially, pick your battles and don’t overreact to small criticisms such that they become big news.

**Supporting Quote:** “The more you try to hide something, the more visible it becomes.” – public relations adage (a lay summary of the Streisand effect) . Information age lore is filled with examples: a single tweet or video, once “forbidden,” ends up reprinted or linked by hundreds of outlets.

**Related Bias:** Reactive Curiosity – people have a bias to seek out information that is being kept from them (forbidden fruit effect). This plays into the Streisand effect. By understanding this, reputation managers often choose to address issues frankly and get ahead of the narrative, rather than attempting a hush-up that would pique public curiosity.
