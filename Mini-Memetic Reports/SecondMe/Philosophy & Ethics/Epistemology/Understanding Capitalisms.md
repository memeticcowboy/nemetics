# Understanding Capitalism's Foundations: Insights from Michael Perelman's The Invention of Capitalism

Created at 2025/07/03 6:31 AM

We cannot repattern what we do not understand. If we want to transform the ways we coordinate our lives, our labor, and our collective futures, then we must first trace how the current patterns came to be. This is not a matter of abstract critique. It is a practical necessity.

Michael Perelman's The Invention of Capitalism offers one of the clearest windows into this foundational work. At its core, the book is not merely about capitalism's economic machinery. It is about the architecture of forced coordination. Perelman carefully documents how the celebrated thinkers of classical political economy like Adam Smith, David Ricardo, James Steuart, and others, publicly argued for laissez-faire markets while privately or practically advocating for deeply coercive systems of labor discipline.

These economists did not simply describe capitalism. They helped build its foundations. And those foundations were not formed through freedom. They were constructed through dispossession. Through the enclosure of land. Through the criminalization of subsistence. Through poor laws and workhouses designed not to alleviate suffering but to push the rural poor into wage labor. The "invisible hand" was only made possible by the very visible fist of law and policy.

Perelman's contribution is especially valuable because it shatters the myth that capitalism emerged from voluntary exchange or spontaneous order. Instead, he shows that classical economics served as a kind of ideological scaffolding. It provided elegant justifications for a system that was brutal in its implementation. In private letters and policy memos, many of these early economists acknowledged the harshness of these measures. But in their published works, they smoothed over these contradictions in the name of efficiency and progress.

To understand this is not merely to indict a historical moment. It is to recognize a pattern. Systems of domination do not only arise through force. They are maintained through stories, through models, and through frames of intelligibility that mask coercion as necessity. If the original coordination of capitalism required enclosure and internalization, then any effort to transform those systems must begin with an equally deep grasp of how they were built.

This is the task before us now. The future will not emerge from better policy alone. It will require a metabolic shift in how we pattern coordination itself. A new architecture of relating. A different logic of value. And crucially, a confrontation with the inherited ghosts of economic thought that still shape our institutions, often without our awareness.

Perelman's book is not just a critique. It is a signal. It invites us to stop mistaking historical outcomes for natural inevitabilities. It reminds us that systems can be re-patterned, if we are willing to do the work of remembering how they were woven in the first place.

We do not begin in a vacuum. We begin in a fabric already threaded. If we want to weave differently, we must first learn where the knots were tied.
