# Wittgenstein: Language's Limits Shape Our Reality

Created at 2025/11/07 11:36 AM

The quote, "**The limits of my language mean the limits of my world**" ("Die Grenzen meiner Sprache bedeuten die Grenzen meiner Welt" in the original German), was famously stated by the Austrian-born philosopher **Ludwig Wittgenstein**. 

It appears as proposition **5.6** in his seminal work, the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1922). 

Meaning and Context

In the context of the *Tractatus*, the quote has a specific and technical meaning tied to Wittgenstein's logical picture theory of language: 

- **"World" as facts:** For Wittgenstein, the "world" (my world) is the totality of facts that can be described by logical, truth-valued propositions. It is not merely the sum total of all existing things, but the sum of all *possible* states of affairs that can be expressed in language.
- **"Language" as propositions:** Language, in this context, is the totality of those logical propositions.
- **The Inseparability of the two:** The statement means that what can be conceived and understood—what constitutes one's reality in a meaningful, factual sense—is constrained by the structure and limits of the language one uses to think and speak about it. There is nothing that can be thought of as outside of language that still constitutes a "fact" in this logical sense. 

Essentially, the boundaries of language define the boundaries of comprehensible reality. If something cannot be put into words (i.e., expressed as a logical proposition), it cannot be coherently thought about as a fact within this framework, and therefore, it is outside the limits of one's world. 

The "Unsayable"

It is important to note, however, that for Wittgenstein, the limits of language do not mean everything outside them is unimportant. On the contrary, he concluded the *Tractatus* by saying: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence" (Proposition 7). The "unsayable" things—such as ethics, aesthetics, and the mystical aspects of life—were of the highest importance to him, even if they could not be expressed in factual propositions. His early philosophy sought to draw a clear line between what could be said (science/facts) and what could only be shown (values/ethics/aesthetics).
