# The Role of Epistemology in Economic Interpretation and Data Storytelling

Created at 2025/06/12 8:52 PM

No, economics is not *just* telling stories with data based on a shared epistemology derived from preferred schools of thought, but there’s some truth to the idea. Let’s break it down.

**What’s Epistemology in Economics?**

Epistemology is the study of how we know what we know. In economics, different schools of thought—like **neoclassical**, **Keynesian**, or **Marxist**—each have their own epistemological foundations. These frameworks shape how economists interpret economic phenomena and use data. For instance:

- A **neoclassical economist** might view unemployment data as evidence of market inefficiencies or supply-demand imbalances.
- A **Marxist economist** might interpret the same data as a symptom of systemic exploitation under capitalism.

The data is the same, but the “story” changes depending on the lens.

**Storytelling with Data?**

The phrase “telling stories with data” implies that economists select and present data to fit a narrative aligned with their school’s perspective. There’s some merit to this:

- Economists often start with a theoretical framework (e.g., supply and demand for neoclassicals, or aggregate demand for Keynesians).
- They then use data to support or refine that framework, which can look like crafting a story.

For example, during a recession, a Keynesian might highlight falling consumer spending and argue for government intervention, while a neoclassical economist might focus on labor market rigidities and advocate for deregulation. Same economic event, different narratives.

**More Than Just Stories**

However, economics isn’t *only* storytelling. It’s grounded in rigorous methodology:

- **Econometrics**: Statistical tools test hypotheses and measure relationships (e.g., does raising the minimum wage reduce employment?).
- **Models**: Formal frameworks (like supply-demand curves or IS-LM models) aim to predict outcomes, not just narrate them.
- **Peer Review**: The field subjects claims to scrutiny, pushing for objectivity.

This distinguishes economics from pure storytelling—it’s not just opinion or rhetoric; it’s a systematic attempt to understand reality through data and theory.

**The Catch: Interpretation Matters**

That said, the interpretation of data is undeniably influenced by a school’s epistemology. No economist is a blank slate—assumptions about human behavior (rational actors? class struggle?) guide what data they prioritize and how they explain it. This is where the “shared epistemology” part of your question comes in: within a school, economists often share a worldview that shapes their conclusions.

**So, Is It Just Storytelling?**

Not quite. Economics blends empirical analysis with theoretical interpretation. While the influence of different schools can make it *resemble* storytelling—especially when data is framed to fit a narrative—it’s not the whole picture. The field strives for rigor and predictive power, even if epistemological biases play a role.

In short: **Economics relies on data and rigorous methodology, but the interpretation of that data is influenced by the theoretical frameworks of different schools of thought, which can indeed give it a storytelling flavor.** It’s a complex interplay, not a simple label.
