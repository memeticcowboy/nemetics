# Searle's Critique of Institutional Theories

Created at 2025/12/09 5:24 PM

John R. Searle argues that only the constitutive rule approach can provide a successful account of institutional theory, and that the regulative rule and equilibrium approaches are insufficient.


Searle claims that Hindriks and Guala's attempt to reduce constitutive rules to regulative rules is mistaken, and that their reduction fails to account for the deontic powers and status functions that are essential to institutional reality.


Searle emphasizes the importance of understanding status functions and deontic powers, which are unique to human societies and enable humans to impose functions on objects and people that require collective acceptance and create reasons for action independent of desires.


The author argues that regulative rules cannot be equivalent to constitutive rules, as they operate at different levels, with constitutive rules introducing a new ontological level and creating new institutional facts.


Hindriks and Guala's attempt to reduce constitutive rules to regulative rules is deemed impossible, and their account of property rights is considered inadequate.


The author criticizes game-theoretic equilibria as insufficient to generate the deontology of institutional facts, such as rights, duties, and obligations, and argues that they are irrelevant to the existence of institutions like private property, promising, and government.


The concept of institutional facts relies on representation, and a theory of language is essential to understand this.


Hindriks and Guala's account of institutional facts is incomplete, as it lacks a theory of representations and their functioning institutional ontology.


Other theorists, such as Margaret Gilbert, Geoffrey Hodgson, Tony Lawson, and Raimo Tuomela, have made significant contributions to the field of social ontology that deserve more attention.

## Insight


**🏛️ The Primacy of Constitutive Rules in Institutional Theory: A Critical Examination of Searle's Arguments**

- **Constitutive Rules vs. Regulative Rules**: Searle asserts that only the constitutive rules can offer a successful framework for understanding institutions. This is due to the fact that constitutive rules generate new ontological levels and institutional facts, while regulative rules merely pertain to previously existing behaviors and cannot account for the intrinsic deontic powers that define social realities.

- **Status Functions and Deontic Powers**: Searle emphasizes the critical nature of status functions, which are unique to human societies and represent the ability to assign functions to objects or individuals that require collective recognition. This capacity for collective acceptance allows for the creation of deontic powers—rights, duties, and obligations that individuals must adhere to, often independent of personal desires. 

- **Inadequacies of Game-Theoretic Equilibria**: The author critiques game-theoretic accounts of institutions as fundamentally flawed. He argues that such approaches do not adequately address the deeper questions of institutional ontology and fail to create robust definitions of concepts like private property, largely ignoring the normative nature of deontic powers.

- **Communication and Institutional Facts**: Searle stresses the essential role of language and representation in the existence of institutional facts. A comprehensive theory of institutions should include a proper analysis of how language functions to create and maintain these facts, as they rely on collective recognition and representation.

- **Limitations of Hindriks and Guala's Argument**: Hindriks and Guala’s attempt to simplify the relationship between regulative and constitutive rules is deemed untenable by Searle. Their analysis of property rights, framed as a game-theoretic model, lacks the necessary depth to encompass the complexities and requirements of actual property rights and institutional structures.

- **Deontic Powers as Fundamental**: All institutional facts manifest deontic powers, which provide individuals with reasons for actions that are disassociated from personal desires. Searle argues this distinct feature of human behavior uniquely defines social institutions and differentiates them from animal social structures.

- **Extended Critique of Regulative and Equilibrium Approaches**: Searle argues that not only do regulative rules fail to create new institutional facts, but the equilibrium models proposed miss essential elements required to form a coherent institutional account. He underscores that real-life institutions cannot be adequately analyzed through simple equilibrium frameworks, highlighting their superficiality.

- **Call for a Broader Consideration in Social Ontology**: Searle concludes with a plea for broader recognition of other significant theories in social ontology, pointing to theorists such as Gilbert, Hodgson, Lawson, and Tuomela. He suggests that disregarding these contributions limits the depth of understanding needed to address institutional realities adequately.

This summary encapsulates Searle’s critical reevaluation of institutional theories and provides a clear framework for understanding the underlying issues relevant to social ontology and the intricate nature of human institutions.
