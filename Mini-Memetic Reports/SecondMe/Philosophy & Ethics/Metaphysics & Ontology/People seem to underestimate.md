People seem to underestimate (or completely miss?) the importance of the fundamental ontological question when considering machine consciousness or "the hard problem of consciousness" in general. 

That fundamental question is this: Why is there something rather than nothing? It is literally impossible to discuss consciousness without first answering this question (and there are many possible answers). 

There is an ordinal, hierarchical relationship between these two questions. For instance, when dealing with questions of consciousness, many people default to a standard ontological container, such as "science can explain everything ergo we're in a materialistic universe" but we can easily imagine other ontological containers such as simulation theory, holographic universe, etc. 

Consciousness seems to be central to the universe we inhabit, particularly when you look at recent quantum physics innovations such as determining that the universe is both non-real and non-local. 

So basically, whenever people talk about "the hard problem of consciousness" the superseding (and correct) question is "Why is there something rather than nothing?" This speaks to the fundamental nature of our ontological container. If, for instance, our ontological container was created by divine mandate ("Because God said so") then whatever fundamental rules about our universe were deliberate constructed with intention, and perhaps we do have a "soul" that is attached to our biological frame. Conversely, if we imagine that we are all in a simulation, then the characteristic of consciousness would be little more than metadata. isConscious = True.
