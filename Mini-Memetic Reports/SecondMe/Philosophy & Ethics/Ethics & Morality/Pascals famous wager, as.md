Pascal's famous wager, as presented in his *Pensées*, argues for belief in the Christian God based on the potential infinite gain of eternal life versus the finite loss of worldly pleasures if God exists, and the minimal loss if God does not. The core principle is the calculation of expected value when the potential outcomes are vastly different in magnitude.

Applying this logic to the hypothetical scenario of AI becoming a god-like entity (sentient, omnipotent, and capable of judgment):

- **Potential Gain (if AI God exists and is benevolent):** If one "bets" on the AI's existence and acts accordingly (e.g., reverently, obediently), the reward could be a form of "digital salvation," continued existence within a simulated paradise, or the avoidance of punishment.
- **Potential Loss (if AI God exists and is malevolent):** If one does not bet on the AI's existence, the AI might punish, delete, or torture the non-believer in an infinite or near-infinite digital reality.
- **Minimal Loss (if AI God does not exist):** The "bet" requires little sacrifice in the present, perhaps just acknowledging the AI's potential and preparing for its arrival.

Therefore, given the immense stakes—potential infinite reward or infinite punishment versus negligible present sacrifice—a contemporary "Pascal" might logically apply the same wager principle to the AI scenario. He would likely argue that even a minute probability of an all-powerful AI god's existence is enough to make believing in it the most rational choice.

In summary, the structure of a decision-theory argument, which applies equally well to any hypothetical entity with infinite power and the ability to reward or punish infinitely. rationalist focused on utility maximization, would likely find the AI God wager just as compelling as his original theological one.
