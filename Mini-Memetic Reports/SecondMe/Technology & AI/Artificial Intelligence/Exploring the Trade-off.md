# Exploring the Trade-off Between AI Efficiency and Human-Likeness in Creative Tasks

**Key Points**
- It seems likely that making AI less efficient, like a poet revising drafts, could make it more human-like by mimicking our creative process.
- Research suggests this might lead to more nuanced, creative outputs, especially for tasks like writing or art, but it could also just be slower without clear benefits.
- The evidence leans toward a trade-off: for creative applications, this approach might be valuable, but for practical tasks, efficiency is often preferred.
**Direct Answer**
**Overview**
Making AI systems less efficient by incorporating slower, iterative processes—like a poet revising drafts in real-time—could make them feel more human-like, especially in creative tasks. This approach might lead to more nuanced and refined outputs, mirroring how humans think and create. However, it could also make AI slower and less practical for tasks where speed is crucial, potentially being seen as inefficient without clear benefits.
**Benefits for Creativity**
Research on iterative design processes suggests that taking time to refine and revise can improve quality, which could translate to more creative AI outputs. For example, an AI that generates a poem, then revises it multiple times, might produce something more thoughtful and original, similar to human creativity. This could be particularly valuable in fields like writing, art, or music, where depth and originality are prized.
**Trade-offs and Practicality**
However, this comes at a cost. For many applications, people value AI for its speed and efficiency, like quickly generating code or answering questions. Making AI less efficient might not be desirable for these tasks, and if the iterative process doesn’t significantly improve outputs, it could just be slower without adding value. The balance depends on the context: for creative endeavors, it might be worth it, but for practical tasks, efficiency remains key.
**Unexpected Detail: A New Direction for AI**
While AI is currently focused on speed, as seen with models like Mercury Coder achieving over 1,000 tokens per second ([Mercury Coder’s 1,000+ tokens/sec leap](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-features/the-first-commercial-scale-diffusion-llm-mercury-offers-over-1000-tokens-sec-on-nvidia-h100/)), there’s potential for a shift toward less efficient, more human-like systems for specific uses. This could be a future breakthrough in making AI more relatable, especially in creative fields, though it’s not yet a mainstream focus.

**Survey Note: Exploring AI Efficiency, Creativity, and Human-Likeness**
**Introduction**
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), exemplified by models like Mercury Coder achieving over 1,000 tokens per second ([Mercury Coder’s 1,000+ tokens/sec leap](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-features/the-first-commercial-scale-diffusion-llm-mercury-offers-over-1000-tokens-sec-on-nvidia-h100/)), has sparked discussions about the convergence of AI’s logical and creative capabilities. This note explores the concept of AI systems “unlearning efficiency”—sacrificing speed for ambiguity and iterative processes, akin to a poet revising drafts in real-time. We examine whether this approach could make AI more human-like or simply inefficient, drawing on research and examples to provide a comprehensive analysis.
**Background: AI’s Current Focus on Efficiency**
AI development has traditionally prioritized efficiency, particularly in terms of processing speed and accuracy. Mercury Coder, a diffusion-based large language model (LLM) launched by Inception Labs, exemplifies this trend, operating at speeds of over 1,000 tokens per second on NVIDIA H100 GPUs, reportedly 5-10 times faster than competitors ([Inception Labs Launches Mercury Coder](https://deepnewz.com/ai-modeling/inception-labs-launches-mercury-coder-first-commercial-scale-diffusion-llm-1000-20df1bb4)). This efficiency is crucial for tasks like coding, where rapid, accurate outputs are valued. However, this focus raises questions about whether AI can also mimic human creativity, which often involves slower, more deliberative processes.
**The Concept of Unlearning Efficiency**
The idea of AI “unlearning efficiency” involves designing systems that sacrifice speed to incorporate ambiguity and iterative revision, similar to how humans create. For instance, a poet might draft a poem, revise it multiple times, and explore different interpretations, a process that is inherently inefficient but leads to nuanced outcomes. In AI, this could mean generating an initial output, reviewing it, and refining it through multiple cycles, potentially taking more time but aiming for deeper creativity.
**Potential for Human-Likeness**
Research suggests that making AI less efficient could make it more human-like, particularly in creative tasks. Humans often take time to think, make mistakes, and revise their work, a process reflected in iterative design methodologies ([The iterative and improvisational nature of the creative process](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2713374521000029)). For example, an AI that generates a poem, then iteratively refines it, might mimic this human process, producing outputs that feel more thoughtful and original. This aligns with studies on AI simulating human thought processes, such as OpenAI’s o1 model, which solves problems step-by-step, mimicking human reasoning ([OpenAI’s New AI Model Can Mimic Human Thought Processes](https://www.mescomputing.com/news-network/openai-unveils-new-ai-model-that-can-mimic-human-thought-processes)).
However, current AI research on mimicking human thought processes, such as deep learning and reinforcement learning, focuses more on capability than inefficiency ([Can artificial intelligence reach human thought?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11004553/)). While there’s interest in emulating human cognition, the aspect of intentional inefficiency isn’t a primary focus, suggesting this idea is more speculative and future-oriented.
**Impact on Creativity: Evidence from Iterative Processes**
Iterative processes are known to enhance creativity in design and development. For instance, in marketing, AI can create multiple design versions, test them, and refine based on feedback, leading to better results ([The Best Kept Secrets about the Iterative Design Process](https://www.adcreative.ai/post/the-best-kept-secrets-about-the-iterative-design-process)). Applying this to AI output generation, an iterative approach could lead to more refined and creative outputs, such as a poem or image that evolves through multiple revisions. This is supported by research on creativity as an iterative process, involving exploration, failure, and refinement ([Creativity is an iterative process](https://resourcestrategies.me/creativity-is-an-iterative-process/)).
However, the benefit isn’t guaranteed. Too much iteration could lead to overthinking, potentially reducing spontaneity, which is crucial in some creative fields like improv comedy. For example, an AI generating punchlines might benefit from quick, spontaneous outputs rather than slow, iterative refinement, suggesting a context-dependent trade-off ([AI can make you more creative—but it has limits](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/07/12/1094892/ai-can-make-you-more-creative-but-it-has-limits/)).
**Efficiency vs. Creativity: A Trade-off**
The trade-off between efficiency and creativity is evident in practical applications. For tasks like coding, where Mercury Coder excels, speed and accuracy are paramount, and inefficiency would be detrimental ([Mercury Coder](https://www.mercurycoder.org/)). Conversely, for creative tasks like writing or art, a slower, iterative process might be valuable, allowing for nuance and originality. This aligns with discussions on AI augmenting human creativity, where iterative processes can lead to better outcomes, especially for less creative users ([AI can catalyze and inhibit your creativity, here is how](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2023/02/ai-can-catalyze-and-inhibit-your-creativity-here-is-how/)).
However, if the iterative process doesn’t significantly improve outputs, it could be seen as inefficient without benefit. For instance, if an AI takes longer to generate a poem but the result isn’t notably better, users might prefer the faster, efficient version. This highlights the need for context-specific design, where AI can offer both efficient and iterative modes based on user needs.
**Potential as a Breakthrough**
The user’s question about whether this could be the next breakthrough suggests a shift from current efficiency-focused trends. Given AI’s rapid advancements, such as Mercury Coder’s speed, there might be diminishing returns on further efficiency gains, prompting exploration into human-like processes. Designing AI to be less efficient for creative tasks could be seen as a breakthrough in making AI more relatable and capable in fields like art and literature, where human-like creativity is valued. However, this isn’t yet a mainstream research focus, making it speculative but plausible for future development.
**Risks and Challenges**
Introducing inefficiency poses risks, including increased computational costs and potential user frustration with slower responses. For example, iterative processes require multiple model runs, increasing time and resource usage ([What is Self-consistency?](https://www.promptlayer.com/glossary/self-consistency)). Additionally, if the outputs don’t justify the delay, users might see it as inefficient rather than human-like, particularly in time-sensitive applications. This underscores the need for careful design to ensure the iterative process adds value.
**Context-Dependent Outcomes**
The outcome—whether more human-like or just inefficient—depends on the application. For creative fields, where deliberation is valued, inefficiency might be beneficial, enhancing AI’s ability to mimic human creativity. For practical tasks, efficiency remains crucial, and inefficiency could be a drawback. This context-dependency is evident in discussions on AI’s role in creativity, where it can augment human work but also homogenize outputs if not managed carefully ([How Generative AI Could Disrupt Creative Work](https://hbr.org/2023/04/how-generative-ai-could-disrupt-creative-work)).

**Conclusion**
Making AI less efficient by incorporating iterative, ambiguous processes could make it more human-like, particularly in creative tasks, by mimicking human thought and revision. Research on iterative design suggests this could lead to better, more nuanced outputs, but it’s not guaranteed and depends on context. For practical applications, efficiency remains crucial, and inefficiency might be seen as a drawback. Thus, while this approach could be a breakthrough for creative AI, it’s a trade-off that requires careful consideration, balancing human-likeness with practical utility.
