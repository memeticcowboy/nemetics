# Michael Polanyi’s Philosophy on Tacit Knowledge and Its Implications for AI and Automation

Created at 2026/01/21 3:27 PM

Michael Polanyi’s philosophy offers a profound critique of the idea that all knowledge can be digitized or automated. By distinguishing between tacit and explicit knowledge, Polanyi highlights a fundamental limitation of AI and algorithmic logic: the "gap" between what we do and what we can explain.<br>1. The Core Thesis: "We know more than we can tell"<br>Polanyi’s most famous aphorism from The Tacit Dimension (1966) suggests that the foundation of all knowledge is personal and deeply rooted in our physical experience.<br> * Explicit Knowledge: This is information that can be codified, written down, and transmitted via data or manuals. It is the language of algorithms.<br> * Tacit Knowledge: This is "quiet" knowledge. It includes "know-how," intuition, and the mastery of a craft. It is acquired through practice and immersion, not just by reading instructions.<br>The Classic Example: Riding a Bicycle<br>If you ask a physicist to provide the mathematical equations for the balance required to ride a bike, they can do it. However, reading those equations won't help a child stay upright. The child learns through proprioception (the sense of self-movement and body position). This knowledge is "inarticulable"—the child knows how to do it, but cannot "tell" you the physics of how they are doing it.<br>2. From "Knowledge" to "Knowing"<br>Polanyi shifted the focus from knowledge as an object (something you possess) to knowing as an act (something you do). He identified two levels of awareness that function simultaneously:<br> * Proximal Awareness: The tools or sensations we use to perform a task. (e.g., the feeling of a hammer in your hand).<br> * Distal Awareness: The goal or object we are focused on. (e.g., the nail being driven into the wood).<br>We "dwell in" the proximal (the hammer) to attend to the distal (the nail). Polanyi argued that when we try to make the proximal explicit (by looking only at our hand instead of the nail), our performance falls apart. This is often called "paralysis by analysis."<br>3. The Algorithmic Conflict<br>The "flattening" you mentioned occurs because algorithms require codification. To a computer, if it cannot be measured, it does not exist. This creates several tensions:<br>| Dimension | Human Knowing (Tacit) | Algorithmic Logic (Explicit) |<br>|---|---|---|<br>| Context | Relies on "gut feel" and nuances. | Relies on standardized data points. |<br>| Transfer | Passed through apprenticeship/mentorship. | Passed through code and data sets. |<br>| Flexibility | Can adapt to "black swan" events. | Struggles with scenarios outside the training data. |<br>| Judgment | Moral and aesthetic "weight." | Mathematical optimization. |<br>The Doctor’s "Gut Feel"<br>A seasoned doctor might walk into a room and sense a patient is in trouble before looking at the heart monitor. This is not magic; it is the subconscious processing of thousands of tiny cues—skin pallor, the smell of the room, the patient’s posture. An algorithm can analyze the blood pressure data (explicit), but it "flattens" the holistic, sensory context that the doctor’s tacit knowledge provides.<br>4. Why This Matters Today<br>In the age of Large Language Models (LLMs), Polanyi’s work is more relevant than ever. While AI can mimic the output of human knowledge (text, art, code), it lacks the embodiment that Polanyi argued is the source of true meaning.<br>If we rely solely on algorithms to make decisions (in law, medicine, or hiring), we risk losing the "personal participation" of the knower, leading to systems that are technically accurate but contextually "blind."<br>Would you like me to explore how Polanyi’s theories apply specifically to the current "alignment problem" in Artificial Intelligence?<br>
