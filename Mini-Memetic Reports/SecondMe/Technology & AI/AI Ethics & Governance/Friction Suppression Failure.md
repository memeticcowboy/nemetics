# Friction Suppression Failure Mode (FSFM): Challenges in Scalable AI Governance Tools

Created at 2026/01/11 8:12 AM

The Friction Suppression Failure Mode (FSFM)<br><br>A General Failure Mode of AI Governance Tools<br><br>⸻<br><br>1. Definition (Formal)<br><br>Friction Suppression Failure Mode (FSFM):<br>A governance failure in which AI ethics, accountability, or oversight mechanisms lose effectiveness as they become optimized for scalability, institutional comfort, and procedural efficiency—systematically suppressing the very social, political, and epistemic friction required to surface power asymmetries and prevent harm.<br><br>In short:<br><br>The more a governance tool scales smoothly, the less truth it can carry about power.<br><br>⸻<br><br>2. Core Mechanism (Causal Chain)<br><br>FSFM follows a predictable sequence:<br>	1.	Ethical Diagnosis Introduced<br>	•	Tool identifies real asymmetries (power, harm, exclusion, historical injustice).<br>	•	Requires contextual judgment, disagreement, and slowdown.<br>	2.	Institutional Adoption Pressure<br>	•	Organizations seek legitimacy, speed, and risk minimization.<br>	•	Tool is reframed as process rather than intervention.<br>	3.	Friction Reduction<br>	•	Open-ended questions → checklists<br>	•	Deliberation → documentation<br>	•	Stakeholder conflict → representation proxies<br>	•	Judgment → compliance<br>	4.	Automation & Formalization<br>	•	Tool becomes routinized, auditable, and delegable.<br>	•	Human confrontation with trade-offs is removed.<br>	5.	Symbolic Success, Substantive Failure<br>	•	Governance artifact persists.<br>	•	Power relations remain unchanged.<br>	•	Harms recur under compliant appearance.<br><br>⸻<br><br>3. Structural Conditions That Trigger FSFM<br><br>FSFM reliably emerges when three conditions coincide:<br><br>A. Scale Requirement<br><br>The tool must operate across:<br>	•	Multiple teams<br>	•	Multiple products<br>	•	Multiple jurisdictions<br><br>→ Context sensitivity becomes a liability.<br><br>B. Legitimacy Incentive<br><br>The tool is used to:<br>	•	Signal responsibility<br>	•	Satisfy regulators<br>	•	Reduce reputational risk<br><br>→ Appearance of governance outweighs governance itself.<br><br>C. Asymmetric Power Environment<br><br>Affected groups lack:<br>	•	Veto power<br>	•	Enforcement capacity<br>	•	Economic leverage<br><br>→ Ethics becomes advisory rather than constraining.<br><br>⸻<br><br>4. What Gets Selectively Preserved vs. Lost<br><br>Preserved (Scales Well)	Lost (Does Not Scale)<br>Terminology	Judgment<br>Framework names	Conflict<br>Principles	Historical specificity<br>Documentation	Moral accountability<br>Metrics	Lived experience<br>Audits	Consequences<br><br>This explains why labels survive but methods die.<br><br>⸻<br><br>5. Why “Politicized” or “Casuistry-Based” Tools Are Most Vulnerable<br><br>Tools that:<br>	•	Center exemplars<br>	•	Require local history<br>	•	Surface disagreement<br>	•	Force naming of winners and losers<br><br>…are structurally anti-scalable.<br><br>They:<br>	•	Create delays<br>	•	Generate internal dissent<br>	•	Produce discoverable liabilities<br>	•	Resist automation<br><br>Thus, they trigger organizational immune responses.<br><br>⸻<br><br>6. Distinction from Related Failure Modes<br><br>FSFM is not:<br>	•	Ethics Washing (alone)<br>→ That is intentional misuse.<br>FSFM also occurs under good faith.<br>	•	Regulatory Capture (alone)<br>→ That is external power domination.<br>FSFM arises from internal optimization.<br>	•	Bureaucratization (alone)<br>→ FSFM is specifically about the removal of moral and political friction, not just paperwork.<br><br>FSFM explains why even well-designed tools decay after adoption.<br><br>⸻<br><br>7. Diagnostic Indicators (How to Spot FSFM Early)<br><br>A governance tool is entering FSFM if:<br>	•	Questions become “guidance”<br>	•	Case studies become “examples”<br>	•	Stakeholder engagement becomes “consultation”<br>	•	Outputs are evaluated for completeness, not consequence<br>	•	Tools are praised for speed, clarity, and ease of use<br>	•	No one can point to a deployment that was stopped<br><br>The strongest indicator:<br><br>When no one is angry anymore, but nothing has changed.<br><br>⸻<br><br>8. Implications for AI Governance Design<br><br>FSFM implies a hard constraint:<br><br>You cannot simultaneously maximize scale, speed, and power sensitivity.<br><br>Any governance system must choose which it sacrifices.<br><br>Design Consequences<br>	•	Some governance tools must be intentionally unscalable<br>	•	Friction must be protected, not minimized<br>	•	Human judgment must be non-delegable<br>	•	Certain decisions must remain slow, local, and contestable<br><br>Otherwise, governance becomes indistinguishable from compliance theater.<br><br>⸻<br><br>9. Meta-Stability Insight<br><br>FSFM is not a bug—it is a selection effect.<br><br>Institutions select governance tools that:<br>	•	Reduce uncertainty<br>	•	Preserve authority<br>	•	Maintain throughput<br><br>Tools that threaten these conditions are tolerated only at the margins (public sector pilots, NGOs, research).<br><br>⸻<br><br>10. Compact Formal Statement<br><br>You can summarize the failure mode as:<br><br>AI governance fails when mechanisms designed to expose power are forced to operate inside institutions optimized to conceal it.<br><br>If you want, the next step could be:<br>	•	a typology of anti-FSFM design strategies, or<br>	•	a comparison between law-based governance and friction-preserving ethics, or<br>	•	translating FSFM into a red-line test for any proposed AI governance framework.<br><br>Just say which vector you want to push.
