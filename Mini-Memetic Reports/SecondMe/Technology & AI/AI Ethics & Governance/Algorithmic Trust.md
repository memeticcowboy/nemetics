**Central Questions or Problems**

- How do algorithmic systems redefine and measure trust?
- What ethical, relational, and societal dimensions are lost or distorted in current trust models?
- What systemic imperatives (efficiency, control, profit) shape these algorithms?
- What would it take to reimagine trust around dignity, reciprocity, and collective agency?

**3.** 

**Fundamental Concepts**

- Algorithmic Trust: The simulation of trust through quantifiable proxies.
- Trust Proxies: Behavioral signals, reputation, and network metrics used to infer trustworthiness.
- Trustless Systems: Mechanisms (e.g. blockchain) replacing interpersonal trust with computational verification.
- Data as Collateral: Data treated as a form of value in systems of structural scarcity.
- Human-Centered AI (HCAI): A design paradigm emphasizing dignity, critical engagement, and mutual benefit.


**4.** 

**Essential Information**

- Bayesian, graph-based, and ML classifiers are core models of algorithmic trust.
- These models rely on biased priors, performative behavior, and centralized control, often reinforcing inequality.
- “Trustworthiness” is conflated with data richness, social capital, or technical performance.
- Algorithmic systems often exclude marginalized groups due to data fragmentation (data deserts).
- Gaming of trust metrics creates a market of manipulation (e.g., Gaming-as-a-Service).
- Human-centric alternatives focus on participatory design, healthy distrust, and mutual validation.


**5.** 

**Points of View or Frames of Reference**

- Technical/Engineering Frame: Trust as a problem of optimization and performance.
- Ethical/Social Justice Frame: Trust as a human, relational, and moral phenomenon.
- Economic/Systemic Frame: Trust systems as extractive tools aligned with profit and control.
- Decolonial Frame: Algorithmic trust as a form of digital colonialism and objectification.
- Participatory Design Frame: Emphasizing community governance and reciprocal agency


**6.** 

**Underlying Assumptions**

- That trust can be reliably inferred through data-driven proxies.
- That efficiency, precision, and scale are the dominant values in system design.
- That centralized or opaque control can remain ethically neutral.
- That data is a reliable stand-in for human behavior and intent.
- That trust can be “solved” rather than continuously negotiated.

**7.** 

**Inferences and Conclusions**

- Current algorithmic trust models are optimized for systemic throughput, not human dignity.
- These models obscure or invert relational, emotional, and ethical trust.
- Algorithmic systems actively shape societal definitions of “trustworthiness,” reinforcing existing hierarchies.
- A fundamental reorientation is needed—from extractivist trust to relational trust centered on dignity, autonomy, and reciprocity.
