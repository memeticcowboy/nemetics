# Strategic Subtraction: Enhancing Efficiency through Unlearning in Biological and Artificial Systems

Created at 2025/07/29 2:52 PM

Eight Key Thinking Components Underlying

“Assessing Methods of Strategic Subtraction: Spaced Repetitive ‘Unlearning’ and Intentional Pruning”

1.  Purposes and Goals

Overall aim: demonstrate that “less can be more” by showing how deliberate removal—of memories, neural-network parameters, or data influence—improves long-term efficiency, robustness, adaptability, and trustworthiness of both biological and artificial systems

2.  Central Questions or Problems

• How can human memory be adaptively modified or “un-learned” without outright erasure?

• How can artificial models be compressed (pruned) to remain accurate while using fewer resources?

• How can the influence of specific training data be surgically removed from an already-trained model (machine unlearning) for privacy, security, and legal compliance?

• What universal principles or mechanisms connect these seemingly disparate subtraction strategies?

3.  Fundamental Concepts

• Spacing effect & reconsolidation (cognitive)

• Parameter sparsity, scaling laws, over/under-fitting (ML pruning)

• Exact vs. approximate unlearning, right-to-be-forgotten (machine unlearning)

• Strategic subtraction as adaptive optimization across domains (cognitive, AI, gardening, education, organizations)

4.  Essential Information

• Spaced repetition exploits reconsolidation windows to update memories; it is not deletion but overwriting.

• Structured and unstructured pruning algorithms remove weights/neurons/layers; Gradual Magnitude Pruning and MIPP are highlighted techniques.

• Exact unlearning (e.g., SISA) guarantees complete data erasure but is computationally expensive; approximate methods trade completeness for efficiency.

• Cross-domain analogy: pruning a plant or an organization’s initiatives follows the same optimization-through-reduction logic.

5.  Points of View / Frames of Reference

• Cognitive science: memory as dynamic, plastic, and self-optimizing.

• Machine-learning engineering: models as resource-heavy artifacts requiring compression and interpretability.

• Legal-ethical: privacy regulations demand verifiable data erasure.

• Systems theory & biomimicry: universal principle of strategic subtraction for adaptive health.

6.  Underlying Assumptions

• Complex adaptive systems inevitably accumulate redundancies or outdated information.

• Selective removal, if done strategically, improves rather than harms overall performance.

• Biological mechanisms (e.g., reconsolidation) can inspire more efficient artificial “forgetting.”

• Efficiency and interpretability are increasingly critical as models scale.

7.  Inferences and Conclusions

• Reconsolidation-based “unlearning” in humans is an implicit, continuous refinement mechanism.

• Pruning shows that large networks contain sparse sub-networks capable of equal or better performance—challenging the “bigger-is-better” paradigm.

• Current AI architectures are ill-suited for exact, low-cost unlearning; future designs should embed “unlearnability” as a first-class feature.

• Strategic subtraction is a domain-agnostic optimization principle: what is removed is as important as what is retained.

8.  Implications and Consequences

Short-term: immediate gains in model efficiency, privacy compliance, and memory accuracy.

Medium-term: need for new algorithms, metrics, and possibly new hardware that natively support unlearning; ethical frameworks for memory modification in humans.

Long-term: shift in design philosophy for both AI systems (built to forget as easily as they learn) and human-centered technologies (therapies leveraging reconsolidation); broader cultural acceptance of subtraction as a legitimate strategy for growth and health across organizations, education, and personal development.
