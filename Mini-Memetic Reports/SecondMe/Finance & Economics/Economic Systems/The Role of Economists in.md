•  **Inequality**: By treating wealth disparities as a natural outcome of market dynamics.

•  **Environmental Degradation**: By prioritizing GDP growth over sustainability.

•  **Corporate Power**: By advocating policies like deregulation or tax cuts that favor big business.

**Economists Who Challenge Norms**

That said, not all economists fit this mold. Some actively challenge the status quo, offering critical perspectives or alternative systems. Consider:

- **Heterodox Schools**: Marxist economists critique capitalism’s foundations, ecological economists prioritize sustainability over growth, and feminist economists highlight unpaid labor’s role in economies.
- **Notable Figures**: Thomas Piketty’s work on inequality exposes flaws in mainstream thinking, urging systemic change rather than acceptance.

These economists don’t just normalize—they question, disrupt, and propose new norms. Their influence might be smaller compared to mainstream economists, but they show that the profession isn’t monolithic.

**Context Matters**

The role an economist plays often depends on where they work. Those in government, corporate, or think-tank roles might face pressure to align with institutional agendas, nudging them toward normalization. Independent researchers or academics, meanwhile, have more freedom to challenge norms—though even they can be shaped by funding or prevailing paradigms.

**A Nuanced View**

So, are all economists agents of normalization? No. Many do reinforce existing systems, intentionally or not, by working within frameworks that justify the current order. But others resist, offering critiques and alternatives that defy normalization. The question itself is provocative, hinting at a broader debate about economics’ societal role. It’s less about a universal truth and more about recognizing the diversity within the field—and the power economists wield in shaping what we see as “normal.”
