Good question—let’s pull this apart carefully rather than defaulting to the more common usage.

✅ Metacognize

This is the established form. Metacognition means thinking about thinking, and the verb is to metacognize:

“When I read, I try to metacognize by monitoring my comprehension strategies.”

You’ll see this in psychology and education literature:

- “Students who can metacognize perform better.”
- “To metacognize effectively, learners must reflect on their goals and strategies.”


✅ Metacognate

This is not a recognized standard verb. It appears occasionally in informal or experimental writing, but you’ll find almost no reputable dictionary entries or peer-reviewed usage. In other words, metacognate is a neologism—someone’s attempt to create a parallel to cognate (meaning “related by origin”) or perhaps to give it a more process-oriented flair.

Recommendation:

Unless you’re deliberately coining new terms (e.g., in speculative writing, memetic engineering, or philosophical discourse), use “metacognize.”

If you want to push boundaries and invent a new nuance (e.g., metacognate = to form connections between different metacognitive processes), you should define it explicitly so your audience doesn’t assume it’s a typo.

TL;DR:

Stick to metacognize for clarity and academic rigor. Use metacognate only if you intend to introduce a novel conceptual distinction—and be ready to defend that choice.
