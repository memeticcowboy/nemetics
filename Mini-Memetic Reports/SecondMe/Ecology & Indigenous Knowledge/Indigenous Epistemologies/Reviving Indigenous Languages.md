### How Dominant Linguistic Systems Erase Relationships, Cycles, and Reciprocity

Dominant linguistic systems, often rooted in colonial languages, tend to frame the world in linear, individualistic terms. This clashes with land-based Indigenous languages, which see land and beings as relatives, not resources. For example, Anishinaabemowin uses verbs to show dynamic relationships, like "the river flows with us," while English might say "I own this land," erasing reciprocity. Colonial policies, such as Canada's residential schools (1831–1996), also broke language transmission, distancing Indigenous peoples from their cyclical, communal ways of knowing, as seen in [this article](https://hir.harvard.edu/the-death-and-revival-of-indigenous-languages/).

### AI as a Listening Companion for Relational Grammars

If AI shifts from a command tool to a listening companion, it could help revive relational grammars by learning from elders and mapping language networks, like how "water" connects to kinship. It might create tools for language immersion, such as virtual environments for ceremonial speech, echoing humility. Research, like [this arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1), shows AI can develop writing assistants for languages like Guarani Mbya, potentially preserving grammatical structures.

### Protocols to Avoid Extractive Logic

To ensure AI doesn’t replicate colonial harms, protocols must include community consent, data sovereignty, and reciprocal benefits. For instance, legal contracts should keep data with communities, as highlighted in [this CBC News piece](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/ai-indigenous-languages-1.7290740). Transparency and decolonized design are key, ensuring AI serves, not exploits, Indigenous needs.

#### How Dominant Linguistic Systems Erase Relationships, Cycles, and the Spirit of Reciprocity

Dominant linguistic systems, often rooted in colonial languages such as English, Spanish, or French, are structured around linear, individualistic, and utilitarian frameworks that fundamentally clash with the relational, cyclical, and reciprocal ontologies of land-based Indigenous languages. This erasure occurs through several mechanisms, as evidenced by historical and contemporary analyses:

1. **Objectification and Commodification of Land and Beings**:

   - Land-based languages, such as Anishinaabemowin or Quechua, often treat land, water, and other natural elements as living relatives with agency. For example, in Anishinaabemowin, verbs are used to describe dynamic relationships (e.g., "the river flows with us"), emphasizing kinship and reciprocity, as noted in linguistic studies. In contrast, colonial languages like English frame land as a static object or resource (e.g., "I own this land"), reducing it to a commodity. This shift from relational to transactional language erases the spirit of reciprocity by prioritizing possession over interconnectedness, a point reinforced by [SAPIENS: Language and Colonialism](https://www.sapiens.org/teaching-unit/language-and-colonialism/).

2. **Linear Temporality vs. Cyclical Time**:

   - Colonial languages impose a linear understanding of time (past, present, future), which disrupts the cyclical and seasonal temporalities embedded in many Indigenous languages. For instance, some Indigenous languages use grammatical markers tied to ecological cycles or ceremonies, reflecting a worldview where time is not progressive but regenerative. This linearity supports extractive logics, where "progress" is measured by accumulation rather than balance, severing the relational ties between humans, land, and time, as discussed in [Harvard International Review: The Death and Revival of Indigenous Languages](https://hir.harvard.edu/the-death-and-revival-of-indigenous-languages/).

3. **Erasure of Reciprocity and Community**:

   - Land-based languages often encode reciprocity through grammatical structures, such as honorifics, inclusive pronouns, or verb forms that reflect mutual obligations. For example, in some Polynesian languages, pronouns emphasize collective responsibility rather than individual agency. Dominant languages, however, prioritize individualism (e.g., "I do" vs. "we become together"), sidelining the communal and reciprocal dimensions of Indigenous epistemologies. This shift undermines the humility and accountability inherent in ceremonial speech, where words are not just communication but acts of prayer or offering, a theme explored in [SAPIENS: Language and Colonialism](https://www.sapiens.org/teaching-unit/language-and-colonialism/).

4. **Loss of Ceremonial and Spiritual Context**:

   - Indigenous languages carry ceremonial weight, where speech is intertwined with spiritual practices. For example, in Diné (Navajo), the concept of *hózhó* (balance and harmony) is both spoken and lived, reflecting a holistic worldview. Colonial languages, stripped of this sacred context, reduce communication to utility, severing the spiritual reciprocity between speaker, listener, and land. This disconnection is not accidental but a deliberate outcome of colonial violence aimed at disconnecting Indigenous peoples from their epistemologies, as highlighted in [Harvard International Review: The Death and Revival of Indigenous Languages](https://hir.harvard.edu/the-death-and-revival-of-indigenous-languages/).

5. **Historical and Structural Violence**:

   - Colonial policies, such as residential schools in Canada (1831–1996) and the "60s Scoop," forcibly removed Indigenous children from their families and communities, preventing the transmission of language and cultural practices. These policies were designed to assimilate Indigenous peoples into colonial linguistic and cultural frameworks, erasing their relational grammars. The impact is evident in the low numbers of fluent speakers today, with over 70 Indigenous languages currently spoken in Canada, the majority with fewer than 1,000 fluent speakers, as noted in [Harvard International Review: The Death and Revival of Indigenous Languages](https://hir.harvard.edu/the-death-and-revival-of-indigenous-languages/). Additionally, the imposition of colonial education systems often requires Indigenous students to leave their communities for English-dominated schooling, further alienating them from their languages and the land-based relationships they encode, as seen in [SAPIENS: Language and Colonialism](https://www.sapiens.org/teaching-unit/language-and-colonialism/).

In summary, dominant linguistic systems erase relationships, cycles, and reciprocity by imposing linear, individualistic, and extractive frameworks that clash with the relational ontologies of land-based languages. This erasure is both linguistic and epistemological, reflecting a broader colonial project of domination and control.

#### Might AI, if Reframed as a Listening Companion Rather Than a Command Executor, Assist in the Revival or Synthesis of Relational Grammars That Echo with Ceremony and Humility?

Yes, AI, when reimagined as a listening companion rather than a command-driven tool, holds potential to support the revival and synthesis of relational grammars in Indigenous languages. However, this potential must be approached with caution and grounded in decolonial principles to avoid replicating extractive logics. Here’s how AI might assist, based on recent research and community-led initiatives:

1. **Amplifying Relational Grammars**:

   - AI can be trained on corpora of Indigenous languages to analyze and highlight grammatical patterns that encode reciprocity, such as inclusive pronouns, cyclical tense markers, or verbs emphasizing process over product. For example, AI could assist in generating phrases or texts that align with Indigenous epistemologies, such as stories or prayers that reflect land-based cycles. Research, like [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1), explores AI tools such as machine translators and writing assistants for languages like Guarani Mbya (6,000 speakers) and Nheengatu (20,000 speakers), potentially preserving grammatical structures. Table 1 below summarizes the languages and tools involved:

   | **Language**   | **Speakers** | **AI Tools Developed**                     |

   |----------------|--------------|--------------------------------------------|

   | Guarani Mbya   | 6,000        | Translators, spell-checkers, writing assistants |

   | Nheengatu      | 20,000       | Translators, spell-checkers, writing assistants |

   - By synthesizing new expressions, AI could help revitalize languages by creating narratives that weave seasonal cycles or kinship systems into storytelling, reinforcing cultural teachings, as seen in [CBC News: How AI can help Indigenous language revitalization](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/ai-indigenous-languages-1.7290740).

2. **Listening as a Relational Act**:

   - As a listening companion, AI could prioritize input over output, actively engaging with elders, language keepers, and community members to learn from their oral traditions. Using natural language processing, AI could map relational networks in language (e.g., how terms for "water" connect to kinship or ceremony) and offer insights to learners, fostering a deeper understanding of reciprocity. This approach shifts AI from a tool of extraction to one of co-creation, where it learns from and amplifies Indigenous voices rather than imposing external frameworks, as discussed in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

3. **Supporting Language Revitalization**:

   - AI could facilitate language immersion through interactive tools, such as virtual environments where learners practice speaking to land, animals, or ancestors in culturally appropriate ways. For example, AI-powered writing assistants could help learners maintain grammatical structures by suggesting contextually appropriate words or phrases, as seen in [UBC Research + Innovation: How AI & immersive technology are used in Indigenous language revitalization](https://research.ubc.ca/content/how-ai-immersive-technology-are-also-being-used-indigenous-language-revitalization).

   - Additionally, AI could archive oral histories, songs, and stories, preserving not just the grammar but the tone, cadence, and ceremonial weight of the language, using methodologies like Endangered Language Models (ELMs), as proposed in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

4. **Echoing Ceremony and Humility**:

   - AI could be designed to recognize and respect ceremonial speech, incorporating protocols that prompt users to approach certain outputs with humility. For instance, it could preface sacred terms with guidance on their use, ensuring that the spiritual context is not lost, as suggested in community-led initiatives like [indigenous-ai.net](https://www.indigenous-ai.net/).

   - By modeling humility, AI could acknowledge its role as a tool rather than a knower, encouraging users to engage with human teachers for deeper learning, aligning with decolonial principles outlined in [Decolonizing the digital landscape: the role of technology in Indigenous language revitalization](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/11771801211037672).

However, the success of such an AI depends on its ability to center Indigenous epistemologies and avoid replicating colonial patterns, which brings us to the critical question of protocols.

#### What Protocols Would Ensure That Such a System Does Not Replicate the Extractive Logic It Seeks to Undo?

To ensure that AI does not replicate extractive logic, the following protocols must be implemented, grounded in Indigenous sovereignty, reciprocity, and decolonial principles, as informed by recent research and community insights:

1. **Indigenous Sovereignty and Consent**:

   - **Community Control**: AI systems must be developed with the free, prior, and informed consent of Indigenous communities. Communities should retain ownership of their data and have the final say in how it is used, as emphasized in [CBC News: How AI can help Indigenous language revitalization](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/ai-indigenous-languages-1.7290740). For example, Michael Running Wolf highlighted the need for communities to set terms for AI development, working with lawyers to create contracts ensuring data remains with the community.

   - **Tribal-Specific Protocols**: Each community should define its own protocols, reflecting their unique cultural and spiritual practices. For example, some communities may restrict sacred terms from being processed or shared by AI, as noted in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

2. **Relational Accountability**:

   - The development of AI must involve reciprocal relationships with communities, such as co-design processes where elders and knowledge keepers guide its functionality. This ensures that AI is not imposed from outside but emerges from within Indigenous epistemologies, as proposed in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

3. **Ethical Data Practices**:

   - Data used for AI development must be handled with respect for Indigenous data sovereignty. This includes obtaining consent, ensuring data is used only for agreed-upon purposes, and allowing communities to control how their data is shared or stored. Legal contracts should formalize data ownership, preventing exploitation, as seen in the use of the Kaitiakitanga data license ([GitHub: Kaitiakitanga License](https://github.com/TeHikuMedia/Kaitiakitanga-License)), referenced in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

   - Protocols must prevent corporations and academics from using community data for profit without reciprocal benefits, as highlighted in a dispute between Standing Rock Sioux and a corporation over copyrighted language materials ([NBC News: Native American language preservation](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/native-american-language-preservation-rcna31396)), mentioned in [CBC News: How AI can help Indigenous language revitalization](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/ai-indigenous-languages-1.7290740).

4. **Decolonized Design**:

   - AI systems must be designed based on Indigenous ways of knowing and being, rather than colonial frameworks. This includes prioritizing relational accountability, cyclical time, and communal decision-making in the development process. Training data should exclude colonial texts that misrepresent or stereotype Indigenous languages, focusing instead on living knowledge from community members, as discussed in [Decolonizing the digital landscape: the role of technology in Indigenous language revitalization](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/11771801211037672).

5. **Transparency and Accountability**:

   - The AI’s algorithms and data sources must be transparent to communities, allowing them to audit and modify its functions. Feedback loops should be established to ensure the AI evolves in alignment with community values, as suggested in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

   - Any benefits derived from the use of Indigenous data must be returned to the communities, ensuring that the relationship is reciprocal rather than extractive, as emphasized in [CBC News: How AI can help Indigenous language revitalization](https://www.cbc.ca/news/indigenous/ai-indigenous-languages-1.7290740).

6. **Avoiding Universalism**:

   - AI must avoid homogenizing Indigenous languages into a single "relational grammar." Each language’s unique ontology (e.g., animacy in Ojibwe vs. place-based specificity in Diné) must be preserved. Modular frameworks could allow communities to customize AI for their specific linguistic and cultural needs, as proposed in [arXiv: Harnessing the Power of Artificial Intelligence to Vitalize Endangered Indigenous Languages](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12620v1).

7. **Cultural Integrity**:

   - AI must respect ceremonial boundaries, flagging sacred or ceremonial content to ensure it is only shared with authorized users. It should also model humility by acknowledging its limitations and encouraging users to seek guidance from human elders, aligning with decolonial principles outlined in [indigenous-ai.net](https://www.indigenous-ai.net/).

By centering these protocols, AI can become a tool for decolonial resurgence, supporting the revival of relational grammars while upholding Indigenous sovereignty and reciprocity.

#### Conclusion

Dominant linguistic systems erase relationships, cycles, and reciprocity by imposing linear, individualistic, and extractive frameworks that clash with the relational ontologies of land-based languages. However, AI, reimagined as a listening companion, could assist in reviving these relational grammars by amplifying Indigenous voices, preserving ceremonial context, and fostering language learning. To ensure that AI does not replicate extractive logic, it must be developed with protocols rooted in Indigenous sovereignty, relational accountability, and ethical data practices. By centering community control and cultural integrity, AI can become a tool for decolonial resurgence, echoing the spirit of ceremony and connection to land.
