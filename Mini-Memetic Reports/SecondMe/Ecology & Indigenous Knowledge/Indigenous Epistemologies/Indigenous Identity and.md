# Indigenous Identity and Spiritual Connection to Land

Here is the extracted text from the image (cleaned up and in proper reading order):<br>“To take the measure of a man one must first take stock of his culture. For what is any man but the sum of his people So . . . what of his people if not connected to the soil beneath their feet.<br>Man’s soul survives in a spiritual partnership with all of creation. His nature tied to the spirit of the earth. To deny this results in being cast about by the winds, lost with no identity.<br>In our early beginnings to “be Indian” was determined by land, language, community and family. There was a great power in this. It is where we received our strength. We signified this with the striking the earth symbol. It was this power that the European society feared. Due to their insatiable appetite for natural resources, gluttony for expansion, and ability to conquer, they themselves had lost this connection.<br>Inflicting a holocaust upon the North American tribes now centuries later they face another cultural threat from the south.”<br>This is a quote widely attributed to Chief Dan George (1899–1981), a Tsleil-Waututh Nation chief, actor, and poet from British Columbia, Canada. The passage reflects his recurring themes of Indigenous identity, connection to the land, and the spiritual consequences of losing that bond, themes he often spoke and wrote about (most famously in his 1967 speech “Lament for Confederation” and his 1974 book My Heart Soars).<br>While the exact wording in this meme varies slightly from verified published versions of his writings, the ideas and much of the phrasing are consistent with Chief Dan George’s known work and philosophy.<br><br>The passage you shared from Chief Dan George centers on a profound Indigenous truth: human identity, dignity, and spiritual wholeness are inseparable from a living, respectful relationship with the land and all creation. When that relationship is severed—through greed, conquest, or domination—both the conqueror and the conquered suffer a kind of soul-loss. Your question beautifully connects this to Scripture, especially the vision of the Peaceable Kingdom in Isaiah 11:1–9, and to the broader biblical lament over humanity’s violence toward animals and the earth.<br>Here are some direct scriptural threads that resonate deeply with both Chief Dan George’s words and the reality of industrial-scale animal suffering today:<br>	1	The Original Harmony and Its Fracture<br>	◦	Genesis 1:29–30 shows the original diet in Eden as plant-based for both humans and animals—no bloodshed.<br>	◦	Genesis 9:4–5 and Hosea 2:18 speak of a covenant God makes not only with humanity but “with the beasts of the field, the birds in the sky and the creatures that move along the ground.” The Indigenous insight that humans become “lost with no identity” when disconnected from creation mirrors the biblical diagnosis: when we break covenant with the animals and the land, we break covenant with God and lose our own humanity.<br>	2	Isaiah’s Peaceable Kingdom (Isaiah 11:6–9) The wolf dwelling with the lamb, the leopard lying down with the goat, the cow and bear feeding together, the child playing near the cobra’s den—“They will neither harm nor destroy on all my holy mountain”—is not just a cute scene. It is the restoration of the original creational intent after human sin introduced predation, fear, and violence into the world. In the renewed creation, the knowledge of the Lord fills the earth “as the waters cover the sea,” and that knowledge necessarily includes knowing how to live without exploiting or slaughtering sentient creatures.<br>	3	The Scope of Redemption<br>	◦	Hosea 4:1–3: Because of human faithlessness, “the land mourns, and all who live in it waste away; the beasts of the field, the birds in the sky and the fish in the sea are swept away.”<br>	◦	Romans 8:19–22: “The creation waits in eager expectation… [it] was subjected to frustration… [it] will be liberated from its bondage to decay.” Scripture refuses to limit redemption to humans alone. The animals we factory-farm, hunt to extinction, or experiment on are included in both the groaning and the final liberation.<br>	4	Jesus and the Animals Early Christians noticed that Jesus, in his 40 days in the wilderness, “was with the wild animals, and the angels attended him” (Mark 1:13)—a brief return to the peaceable kingdom. Many Church Fathers (St. Isaac the Syrian, St. Basil the Great, St. John Chrysostom) taught that the compassion of Christ extends to every sentient creature and that cruelty to animals is incompatible with the age to come.<br>Chief Dan George’s lament—“they themselves had lost this connection”—is almost prophetic when read beside Scripture. European colonial culture, driven by an anthropology that saw the earth and its creatures as mere resources, enacted a spiritual rupture whose consequences we still live with: billions of animals in concentration-camp conditions, oceans acidifying, species vanishing. Isaiah’s vision says this cannot be the final word. The Messiah’s reign will restore the bond, and humans will once again learn to “neither harm nor destroy” any creature on God’s holy mountain.<br>In short, the Indigenous elder and the Hebrew prophet are saying the same thing in different languages: A people who kill and consume billions of God’s sentient creatures cannot possibly be at peace—neither with the land, with the animals, with one another, nor with the Creator. True humanity is only recovered when we recover harmlessness, when the knowledge of the Lord teaches us again how to live gently upon the earth.<br><br>Via [x/ Bob-RJ](https://x.com/burkhartrj/status/1991150862069117049?s=46&t=7Z-E-ACnGlzdI-iyUwNCrQ)

## Insight


* The quote attributed to Chief Dan George highlights the intrinsic connection between identity and the land within Indigenous cultures, emphasizing that understanding a person's essence requires an appreciation of their cultural roots. This ties into the broader context of how colonization disrupted these connections, leading to a loss of identity for many Indigenous peoples, as they were forcibly disconnected from their lands and traditions.

* The reference to the "striking the earth symbol" exemplifies a physical manifestation of cultural identity, representing the strength and power derived from a deep-rooted relationship with the land. This notion resonates with Indigenous belief systems that view nature not as a resource to exploit but as a sacred partner.

* The spiritual and ecological themes found in the quote align with biblical ideas of stewardship and the relationships between humanity, animals, and creation. Specifically, the concept of humans losing their identity when disconnected from nature parallels theological perspectives on covenant in Scripture, particularly as illustrated in Genesis and the prophetic visions of Isaiah.

* This connection is further strengthened by contemporary discussions about environmental ethics and the welfare of sentient beings, as evidenced by the quotes from Pope Leo XIV and David Pearce. Their remarks underscore a growing recognition within religious and ethical discourse of the moral responsibility to protect creation and live harmoniously with all life forms, echoing the sentiments expressed by Chief Dan George.

* The notion of a "peaceable kingdom," as stated in Isaiah, serves as a hopeful vision for both biblical and Indigenous teachings, suggesting a future where humanity respects and lives in harmony with all creatures, thereby reclaiming a lost connection to creation. This speaks to the universality of the message: true peace and identity are woven through our relationships with the land and its inhabitants.
