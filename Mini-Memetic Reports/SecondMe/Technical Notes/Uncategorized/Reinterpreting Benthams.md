# Reinterpreting Bentham's Panopticon: From Secular Utility to Foucauldian Power Metaphor

Created at 2025/10/14 9:24 AM

The idea that Jeremy Bentham's Panopticon "theologizes surveillance as benevolent divine watching" is a modern, critical interpretation, most notably influenced by the work of Michel Foucault, rather than Bentham's own view. While Bentham conceived of the Panopticon as a rational, utilitarian system for social good, Foucault reinterpreted its architecture as a metaphor for the pervasive and controlling power structures of modern society. Bentham's original, secular vision

Jeremy Bentham's Panopticon was an architectural design for a prison that allowed a single guard to observe all inmates without them knowing if they were being watched at any given moment.

- **Utilitarianism**: Bentham, a proponent of utilitarianism, believed the Panopticon would lead to the "greatest happiness for the greatest number" by reforming criminals and preventing crime.
- **Psychological control**: The primary mechanism was psychological. The constant potential for observation would cause inmates to internalize the guard's gaze, compelling them to police their own behavior.
- **Rational and secular**: Bentham intended the Panopticon as an efficient, economical, and humane alternative to the brutal forms of punishment that were common in his era. His justification was entirely secular and based on principles of rational efficiency, not divine judgment. Foucault's theological reinterpretation

In his 1975 book *Discipline and Punish*, Michel Foucault used Bentham's Panopticon as a metaphor for the insidious nature of modern power and surveillance.

- **The "all-seeing" gaze**: Foucault highlighted the architectural feature of the central, unseen tower from which the many are watched, comparing it to an all-seeing, yet invisible, power.
- **An instrument of disciplinary society**: For Foucault, the Panopticon's psychological control represented how modern institutions—including schools, hospitals, and factories—shape "docile bodies" and enforce social norms through hierarchical observation and normalizing judgments.
- **The "divine" metaphor**: Many later analyses, influenced by Foucault, have extended this metaphor to theological dimensions, drawing a parallel between the invisible, omnipresent observer in the tower and the concept of an all-seeing God. This "divine" parallel frames the observer as benevolent, but the power dynamic remains fundamentally about control and normalization. Why the comparison is contested

The statement "theologizes surveillance as benevolent divine watching" captures a critical interpretation of the Panopticon, but it misrepresents Bentham's original intentions.

- **Bentham's goal was control, not salvation**: Bentham's goal was not to save souls but to produce disciplined, productive citizens for the benefit of society. While he believed the system was morally superior to corporal punishment, the goal was social engineering through control of the mind.
- **The benevolence is debatable**: For Foucault and many subsequent thinkers, the surveillance model is not benevolent but a coercive and insidious form of power that strips individuals of their autonomy. By contrast, the biblical concept of divine surveillance is often framed by believers as an act of loving, providential care. The critical use of "benevolent" in the statement is therefore ironic.
