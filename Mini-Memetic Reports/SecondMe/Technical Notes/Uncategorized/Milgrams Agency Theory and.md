# Milgram's Agency Theory and Obedience Dynamics

Created at 2025/09/29 9:05 AM

Milgram's Agency Theory explains obedience through the concept of the 'agentic state', where individuals act as agents on behalf of an authority figure, diffusing responsibility for their actions.


In the agentic state, people may experience moral strain and anxiety due to the conflict between their behavior and personal beliefs, as seen in Milgram's experiment where 65% of participants administered electric shocks up to 450V.


The theory suggests that the agentic state is reinforced through socialization, with people learning to obey authority figures from an early age, and that obedience can have survival value by maintaining social harmony.

## Insight


**🧠 Understanding Milgram’s Agency Theory and Its Role in Explaining Obedience**

---

### 🔍 **Describing the Theory**

- **Key Conclusion:** The 'agentic state' explains high obedience levels seen in Milgram’s experiments.  
  **Explanation:** Participants shifted into the agentic state when perceiving an authority figure's legitimacy, acting on their behalf and believing responsibility was diffused.  
  **Details:** Milgram observed 65% of participants administering electric shocks up to 450V, illustrating obedience driven by the agentic shift rather than personal drive.

- **Key Conclusion:** The 'agentic shift' occurs when individuals recognize authority as legitimate, prompting them to act as agents.  
  **Explanation:** When confronted with authority perceived as legitimate, individuals shift responsibility away from themselves, engaging in potentially destructive actions.  
  **Details:** The shift involves believing the authority is accountable, leading to diffusion of responsibility and reduced personal moral conflict during obedience.

- **Key Conclusion:** The 'autonomous state' involves independent decision-making rooted in personal responsibility.  
  **Explanation:** When not under authority influence, individuals behave according to their moral standards, feeling responsible for their actions.  
  **Details:** Milgram noted that in autonomous states, people act freely without pressures from authority figures, contrasting with the obedient agentic state.

---

### 💡 **Mechanisms Reinforcing the Agentic State**

- **Key Conclusion:** Socialization from childhood reinforces obedience and the propensity to enter the agentic state.  
  **Explanation:** Early experiences—being rewarded for obedience and punished for defiance—instill obedience as a social norm, continuing into adult authority obedience.  
  **Details:** Education and upbringing socialize individuals to accept authority figures, thus preconditioning them to obey in hierarchical contexts.

- **Key Conclusion:** Obedience can have survival value, rooted in evolutionary social hierarchy.  
  **Explanation:** Obedience maintains social harmony and group stability, with natural selection favoring those who comply to avoid conflict.  
  **Details:** The theory posits that this social hierarchy and obedience to authority have historically promoted survival, making individuals more likely to conform under authoritative commands.

- **Key Conclusion:** The agentic shift explains destructive obedience, even where actions conflict with personal morals.  
  **Explanation:** Moral strain and anxiety emerge when individuals harm others under authority orders, but they still obey due to the perceived legitimacy of authority.  
  **Details:** Milgram recorded signs of psychological stress, such as discomfort and anxiety, among participants who inflicted harm, yet they continued obeying.

---

### ⚙️ **Application to Real-Life Situations**

- **Key Conclusion:** The theory helps explain atrocities like the Rwandan Genocide, where individuals obeyed powerful leaders and external authorities.  
  **Explanation:** Perpetrators such as Cyasa Habimana justified killing by viewing themselves as tools of more powerful figures, highlighting agentic obedience.  
  **Details:** Similarly, Paul defied authority orders during the genocide, showcasing how obedience can override moral considerations under influence.

- **Key Conclusion:** Obedience in organizational contexts, such as the MacDonalds scam or military trials, can be explained via agentic state.  
  **Explanation:** Employees or soldiers follow orders due to perceived legitimate authority, often without evaluating moral implications.  
  **Details:** This understanding can shed light on how individuals participate in unethical practices under authority, emphasizing the role of obedience socialization.

- **Key Conclusion:** Situations like Lynndie England’s actions at Abu Ghraib exemplify destructive obedience during authoritative influence.  
  **Explanation:** The soldiers obeyed orders from superiors, which can be explained by their transition into the agentic state, even when actions conflicted with personal morality.  
  **Details:** This suggests that authority-driven obedience can lead to human rights violations, reinforcing the importance of understanding agency dynamics.

---

### 🧪 **Evaluation of Milgram’s Agency Theory**

- **Strength:** **Supported by empirical evidence**, such as the high obedience rate of 65%, illustrating the power of authority in inducing the agentic state.  
  **Details:** Brain imaging research provides objective support that coercion alters neural activity, differentiating it from voluntary behavior.  
  **Impact:** Demonstrates the underlying biological processes involved in obedience, lending credibility to the theory.

- **Weakness:** The theory may **oversimplify obedience**, ignoring individual differences like dispositional factors (e.g., authoritarian personality).  
  **Details:** For example, the authoritarian personality may predispose individuals to obey, regardless of situational factors, which agency theory doesn’t account for thoroughly.

- **Key Concern:** The theory struggles to explain **obedience in the absence of legitimate authority**, such as Jim Jones' Jonestown mass suicide, where charisma and social power played a bigger role.  
  **Details:** Jones lacked traditional authority but relied on referent and charismatic power, challenging the idea that obedience depends solely on legitimate authority.

- **Application Example:** During the Rwanda genocide, obedience was driven by social and political power rather than perceived legitimate authority alone, challenging the scope of the agency theory.

---

### 🎯 **Comparison with Other Theories**

- **Key Conclusion:** Unlike the **Authoritarian Personality Theory**, which attributes obedience to personal dispositional factors, agency theory emphasizes situational influences and socialization processes.  
  **Explanation:** Agency theory focuses on how individuals shift into the agentic state under authority influence, whereas personality traits predispose obedience in authoritarian theory.  
  **Impact:** The debate centers on whether obedience results more from internal traits or external situational pressures.

- **Key Conclusion:** The **Social Power Theory** complements agency theory by explaining how different bases of power (e.g., coercive, referent) influence obedience, expanding beyond the concept of legitimacy.  
  **Explanation:** Power dynamics derived from charisma or social influence can also cause individuals to obey, as exemplified by Jim Jones’ charismatic leadership.

- **Key Conclusion:** **Social Impact Theory** considers group size and immediacy of influence, providing a broader contextual explanation for obedience; it can complement agency theory.  
  **Explanation:** Large authority figures and close proximity increase obedience likelihood, aligning with the idea of social influence driving the agentic shift.

---

### 🔎 **Further Reflection & Implications**

- **Key Conclusion:** Brain imaging research suggests distinct neural activity during coercion, supporting the biological basis of the agentic and autonomous states.  
  **Details:** Caspar (2016) used brain scans to observe different brain processes when individuals obey under coercion versus acting voluntarily, reinforcing that obedience involves specific neural mechanisms.

- **Key Conclusion:** The theory highlights the importance of socialization in establishing obedience patterns early in life, suggesting potential intervention points.  
  **Details:** Educational programs aimed at promoting moral decision-making and resistance to authority could reduce destructive obedience.

- **Key Conclusion:** Milgram’s theory remains relevant in understanding obedience in modern contexts, such as corporate misconduct and military ethics.  
  **Details:** Recognizing the power of authority and social influence can inform policies for ethical conduct, reducing the likelihood of destructive obedience.
