# Strengthening US Data Privacy Against UTS Threats

Created at 2025/10/13 8:55 PM

The FBI's efforts to mitigate ubiquitous technical surveillance (UTS) are insufficient, as highlighted in a report by the Department of Justice's Office of the Inspector General (OIG).


UTS poses a significant threat to US national security, with adversaries using advanced surveillance techniques to collect and analyze data from various sources, including smartphones, wearable devices, and internet-connected sensors.


Broader US data privacy and security protections are needed to reduce the risk of UTS and lessen the mitigation workload for national security operators, as current industry data and digital practices exacerbate the problem.


The U.S. is vulnerable to technical surveillance from foreign adversaries, such as Beijing, who can exploit the country's data-laden environment.


The U.S. government's approach to data privacy and security is fragmented, with a mismatch between policymakers who focus on national security and those who focus on privacy.


Comprehensive data privacy laws and federal regulations could help mitigate the challenges of technical surveillance, such as limiting data leakage from devices and imposing security requirements on industries.

## Insight


**🌐 UTS Threat Overview and Its Impact on U.S. National Security**  
- **UTS is a growing, pervasive threat to U.S. security**: The OIG report highlights that advances in technology make it easier for adversaries, including criminal enterprises and nation-states like China and Russia, to exploit vulnerabilities created by ubiquitous technical surveillance (UTS).  
- **Current mitigation efforts are insufficient**: Despite specialized agency work, the FBI and allied agencies struggle to keep pace with the scale and sophistication of UTS, risking compromising US personnel and operations through real-time data access and exploitation.  
- **Real-world example underscores dangers**: In 2018, a drug cartel used geolocation and camera access—potentially orchestrated via commercial data or system breaches—to target FBI agents, illustrating the severe consequences of UTS failures.  

**🔍 The Role of Private Industry and Digital Practices in Exacerbating UTS Risks**  
- **Industry data collection fuels surveillance vulnerabilities**: Massive data harvesting by private firms—through devices, apps, sensors, and online activities—creates extensive digital exhaust that adversaries can exploit.  
- **Existing piecemeal mitigations are ineffective long-term solutions**: Measures like departmental memos to disable location services do not fundamentally alter underlying data collection mechanisms, which are often default, continuous, and minimally regulated.  
- **Consumers’ lack of control amplifies risk**: The widespread availability of detailed personal data, without effective rights or awareness, enhances adversaries’ ability to surveil both civilians and government personnel.  

**🛡️ Challenges in U.S. Data Privacy, Security, and Fragmented Policy Landscape**  
- **Fragmented government approach hampers comprehensive defense**: Policymakers focused separately on national security and privacy create disconnects, leaving gaps in legislation and enforcement.  
- **Insufficient legal frameworks**: The absence of broad, enforceable data privacy laws and security regulations allows private sector practices—like data hoarding and weak security—to persist, increasing UTS vulnerabilities.  
- **Policymaker and industry inertia**: Lack of cohesive oversight and regulatory standards means adversaries can exploit asymmetries in data control and cybersecurity, making mitigation more difficult for security agencies.  

**🤖 Advancing UTS from Device Level to Infrastructure and Network Systems**  
- **Multiple entry points for surveillance**: Mobile phones, wearables, IoT devices, connected vehicles, and cloud services collectively form a complex ecosystem easily exploited by adversaries for real-time data collection and analysis.  
- **Default, continuous data collection intensifies vulnerabilities**: Many devices and apps automatically harvest and transmit data with minimal user awareness or control, complicating mitigation efforts for personnel operating abroad or in sensitive locations.  
- **Operational challenges**: Addressing these vulnerabilities requires constant updates, training, and tailored mitigation strategies—burdens that are overwhelmed by the scale of private-sector data practices and international infrastructure.  

**🌍 Geopolitical and Technological Backdrop of UTS Risks**  
- **Foreign adversaries leverage extensive surveillance infrastructure**: China’s widespread CCTV, facial and gait recognition, and Russia’s biometric tools exemplify state-level capabilities that threaten U.S. personnel and intelligence activities.  
- **Increased automation and AI integration**: Use of AI to analyze data intensifies the potential for rapid, large-scale surveillance that can identify targets or monitor activities in real time, escalating operational risks.  
- **Mismatched threat perception**: While agencies focus on exploiting technology for intelligence, policies lag behind in addressing how adversaries mirror and expedite such digital surveillance efforts—heightening threat exposure.  

**⚖️ The Legislative and Regulatory Gap in U.S. Data and Privacy Protections**  
- **Disconnect between privacy and security policymaking**: Congressional committees responsible for consumer privacy (Energy & Commerce) and those overseeing national security (Armed Services, Intelligence) operate in silos, impairing comprehensive response.  
- **Regulatory vacuum facilitates adversary advantage**: Weak laws on data collection, sharing, and security enable industry practices that increase UTS risks—such as unregulated real-time bidding or insecure connected devices.  
- **Potential of comprehensive laws**: Implementing robust privacy, data security, and cybersecurity regulation could reduce data leakage, raise hurdles for cyber-espionage, and lower the operational burden on security agencies.  

**💡 Strategic Recommendations for Reducing UTS Risks**  
- **Enact broad data privacy and security legislation**: Federal laws should limit data collection, enforce security standards, and establish consumer rights—cutting down on the digital footprints exploited by adversaries.  
- **Standardize industry security practices**: Sector-specific regulations, including minimum cybersecurity protocols for connected devices and infrastructure, would make UTS targeting more resource-intensive for adversaries.  
- **Bridge policy gaps across agencies**: Harmonize efforts between privacy advocates, security agencies, and lawmakers to develop integrated, enforceable frameworks that protect both civil liberties and national security interests.  
- **Enhance operational mitigations with legal backbone**: Improve training and technical defenses while establishing clear legal standards that restrict pervasive data collection, simplifying threat mitigation for personnel worldwide.  

**🏁 Conclusion**  
Addressing the UTS threat demands proactive, comprehensive federal legislation on data privacy and security, a unified policy approach bridging civilian and security sectors, and industry-wide adoption of robust security standards. These measures will diminish the operational workload for U.S. security agencies, minimize vulnerabilities exploited by adversaries, and fortify the national digital environment against pervasive surveillance threats.
