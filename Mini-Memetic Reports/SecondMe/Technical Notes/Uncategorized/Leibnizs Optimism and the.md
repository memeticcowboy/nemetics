# Leibniz's Optimism and the Problem of Evil

Created at 2025/10/13 8:09 PM

The phrase "the best of all possible worlds" was coined by Gottfried Leibniz in his 1710 work Théodicée to solve the problem of evil and reconcile the existence of evil with a perfectly good, all-powerful, and all-knowing God.


Leibniz argued that God chose to create this world because it has the greatest "fitness" or "perfection" among all possible worlds, and that the presence of evil does not necessarily mean a worse world.


Leibniz's theory was criticized by various philosophers, including Voltaire, Arthur Schopenhauer, Bertrand Russell, and Alvin Plantinga, but has also been defended by philosophers such as William C. Lane, Justin Daeley, and James Franklin.

## Insight


**🌍 The Concept of the "Best of All Possible Worlds" by Leibniz**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** Leibniz coined the phrase to explain God's reason for creating this specific world.  
  - **Explanation:** In his 1710 _Théodicée_, Leibniz proposed that God selected this world because it exhibits the highest degree of "fitness" and perfection among all possible worlds, thus making it the optimal choice.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** The phrase aims to reconcile the existence of evil with God's omnibenevolence and omnipotence.  
  - **Explanation:** By asserting that this is the best possible world, Leibniz suggests that any evil present is a necessary component that possibly enhances overall goodness, fitting into his theodicy framework.  

**🔎 Foundations of Leibniz's Theodicy and the "Best World" Argument**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** The actual world is the result of God's wisdom and goodness, choosing the world with maximum perfection.  
  - **Explanation:** Leibniz emphasizes that God's selection is based on the "fitness" or "perfection" of possible worlds, and that God's knowledge, goodness, and power drive this choice, which ultimately produces the "greatest good".  
- **Key conclusion 2:** Possible worlds are defined as coexistent, logically consistent combinations of beings, known as "compossible" worlds.  
  - **Explanation:** Possible worlds are based on logical consistency where beings and events do not contradict each other, and God's choice among infinite options is constrained by this logical structure.  

**🎭 The Problem of Evil and Its Resolution**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** Evil is not a "thing" but a lack or privation of good, which exists within this world but does not contradict its overall perfection.  
  - **Explanation:** Following a tradition from Augustine, Leibniz sees evil as a secondary absence, and not as created by God, but as an imperfection that is compatible with divine goodness.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** The presence of evil can contribute to greater overall good, making this the "best" world even with suffering.  
  - **Explanation:** Leibniz argues that evil often enhances the discernibility and appreciation of good, and some goods are only possible with accompanying evils ("contingent" goods). Analogies such as bitter flavors making sweetness more perceptible clarify this view.  

**⚖️ Divine Will and Sufficient Reason**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** God's choice of the world is grounded in the Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR), which states nothing happens without a reason.  
  - **Explanation:** Leibniz insists that God's selection of the world was motivated by its maximum fitness, rejecting arbitrary creation, thus making the world a necessary outcome of rational divine choice.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** God's goodness and wisdom cause the world with the highest degree of perfection to exist.  
  - **Explanation:** The perfect divine nature ensures that the world realized is the best possible, aligning with Leibniz's belief that God's attributes naturally lead to this optimal creation.  

**🧠 Nature of Possible Worlds and Their Selection**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** Possible worlds are all logically consistent and coexistent configurations of beings.  
  - **Explanation:** Based on logical coherence, they include worlds with entities like unicorns or contradictions like married bachelors—possible in definition but not in actual existence.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** God chooses the world that optimizes the “fitness” or “perfection,” which is also the simplest and richest in phenomena.  
  - **Explanation:** Leibniz sees the choice driven by the world’s overall excellence, balancing simplicity (hypotheses) against richness (observable facts).  

**📝 Criticisms and Philosophical Debates**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** Leibniz’s optimism was criticized after the Lisbon earthquake and other disasters, seeing the world as overly idealistic.  
  - **Explanation:** Critics like Voltaire lampooned the idea, noting that suffering and evil seem too overwhelming for this to be the best possible world; the term “Panglossian” mocks persistent optimism.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** Philosophers like Schopenhauer, Russell, and Plantinga challenged Leibniz's optimism, arguing that evil’s magnitude implies the world is not the best or that it is logically inconsistent.  
  - **Explanation:** Russell maintained that evil results from metaphysical imperfection, whereas Plantinga contested that a better world with more moral good could exist, questioning Leibniz’s assumptions.  

**🔬 Broader Implications and Usage outside Theodicy**  
- **Key conclusion 1:** Leibniz’s concept influences theories in metaphysics, such as divine simplicity and the fine-tuned universe, and impacts understanding of morality and science.  
  - **Explanation:** His view that the world is the best possible supports arguments for divinely-ordained natural laws and the interconnectedness of goods and evils through mathematical necessity.  
- **Key conclusion 2:** Critics and scholars connect Leibniz’s optimism to scientific and philosophical ideas, including evolution and the calculus of variations, viewing God as a mathematician solving minimization problems.  
  - **Explanation:** Some like Emil du Bois-Reymond extend Leibniz’s idea to justify that the natural world's functioning minimizes necessary evil, aligning with Darwinian evolution, while others criticize the overly optimistic stance based on suffering seen empirically.
