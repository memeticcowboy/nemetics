That phrase “weaponization of information” is a deliberate metaphor that collapses the distance between war and everyday life. When analysts say social media has become a “battlefield,” they’re doing two things at once:

1.  Re-framing civilians as combatants.

Every like, share, or meme is no longer idle chatter; it’s a potential “force multiplier” for some unseen command. The metaphor turns your aunt posting a sketchy link into an unwitting irregular soldier, and the platform itself into contested terrain.

2.  Justifying covert or coercive counter-measures.

Once information is labeled a “weapon,” the state can claim the right to “defend” itself with tools previously reserved for kinetic war—surveillance, disruption, pre-emption, even retaliatory strikes (think account takedowns, bot sweeps, or offensive hacks). The battlefield metaphor provides the legal and moral cover: if you’re under attack, the Geneva Conventions don’t apply.

The irony is that the same agencies pushing the metaphor also exploit it. By insisting the battlespace is “everywhere,” they expand the mandate to operate everywhere. The narrative of ubiquitous threat becomes the warrant for ubiquitous power.
