# Foucault's Analysis of Power: From Sovereign to Disciplinary and Modern Surveillance

Created at 2025/10/14 9:00 AM

Michel Foucault argued that **modern surveillance, or disciplinary power, is a subtle and insidious successor to an older form of power, "pastoral power," which originated in the Christian church**. By appearing to guide and care for individuals, this modern surveillance normalizes behavior and produces "docile bodies"—citizens who regulate themselves according to societal norms. [[1](https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/10150/110194/1/azu_gn1_a785_n13_21_36_w-ocr.pdf#:~:text=According%20to%20Foucault%2C%20political%20order%20is%20maintained,the%20types%20of%20bodies%20that%20society%20requires.), [2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b-UcZn-P_tM), [3](https://pressbooks.pub/surveillancestudies/chapter/disciplinary-society/), [4](https://foucault.info/documents/foucault.power/), [5](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21642850.2015.1049539#:~:text=Using%20this%20concept%20as%20a%20metaphor%2C%20Foucault,spectacle%20of%20sovereign%20power%20of%20previous%20times.)]

The evolution of power: From sovereign to pastoral

According to Foucault, power in Western societies evolved through distinct phases:

- **Sovereign power** involved a visible display of force, such as public executions, to punish crimes against the sovereign ruler. This was an intermittent and absolute power, focused on the body of the condemned.
- **Pastoral power** emerged from Christian institutions and was concerned with the salvation and well-being of the flock. This form of power had four key characteristics:
    - **Concerned with salvation in the next world:** The ultimate aim was to guide individuals toward eternal salvation.
    - **Self-sacrificial:** The shepherd was prepared to sacrifice himself for the flock.
    - **Individualizing:** It did not just concern the collective, but each individual throughout their entire life.
    - **Truth-seeking:** It required knowing the "inside of people's minds" through confession, creating a detailed knowledge of each individual. Modern surveillance as disguised pastoral care

In the modern, secular state, Foucault saw the techniques of pastoral power being repurposed. Instead of salvation in the next world, the state promised worldly "salvation" in the form of health, security, and well-being. This created new "pastors"—doctors, teachers, social workers, and police—who used surveillance and examination to guide and control individual behavior under the guise of care and assistance. Key elements of disciplinary surveillance

Foucault detailed three specific techniques used by institutions to exercise disciplinary power:

- **Hierarchical observation:** The constant visibility of a central authority. The most famous example is the **Panopticon**, a prison design by Jeremy Bentham that allows a central guard tower to watch all prisoners without them knowing if they are currently being observed.
- **Normalizing judgment:** Institutions constantly compare individuals against a standard norm, classifying and judging them. This pressure creates a constant effort by individuals to conform.
- **The examination:** This technique, which combines hierarchical observation with normalizing judgment, produces a "case" file for each individual. A student's exam or a patient's medical history creates an archive that allows authorities to define, classify, and correct an individual. The internalized gaze

The ultimate achievement of modern disciplinary power is that it becomes self-sustaining. The uncertainty of being watched—whether by a prison guard in the Panopticon or an invisible, all-seeing societal gaze—causes individuals to internalize the surveillance. They begin to police their own thoughts and behaviors to conform to the norm, in a "technology of the self" that reinforces the power structure. The result is a society of "docile bodies" that regulate themselves without the need for constant, overt force.
