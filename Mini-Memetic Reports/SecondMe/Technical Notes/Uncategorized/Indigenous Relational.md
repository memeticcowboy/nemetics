### Indigenous Relational Epistemologies: Learning from the More-Than-Human

Robin Wall Kimmerer's integration of Indigenous knowledge systems with botanical science exemplifies traditions that ground cognition in reciprocal relationships with the living world. In *Braiding Sweetgrass*, Kimmerer articulates how Indigenous ways of knowing center relationality, viewing humans as part of rather than apart from natural systems. The Three Sisters agricultural practice—where corn, beans, and squash grow in symbiotic relationship—serves as both farming technique and epistemological model.

Kimmerer's work on the "Honorable Harvest" provides concrete practices for ethical knowledge-gathering that mirror fermentation processes. These practices require asking permission, taking only what is given, minimizing harm, being grateful, and giving back in reciprocity. Knowledge becomes "fermented" through this process of reciprocal exchange, developing complexity and potency over time through continued relationship with the land and its inhabitants.

The Potawatomi concept of "all my relations" extends kinship beyond human boundaries to include "all 'people,' from humans to animals, birds, trees, mountains, and even rocks". This relational ontology treats knowledge as emerging from ongoing dialogue with more-than-human beings, requiring what Kimmerer calls "species loneliness" healing through renewed connection to multispecies communities.
