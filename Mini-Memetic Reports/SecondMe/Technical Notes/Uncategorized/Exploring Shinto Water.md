# Exploring Shinto Water Rituals: Purification Practices and Their Significance

Created at 2025/09/20 2:13 PM

Shinto holds a profound reverence for water as a conduit for purity and renewal—core tenets of *kegare* (impurity) and *harae* (purification). Let me build on this a bit with some context and nuances, drawing from Shinto traditions.

### The Temizuya Ritual: Everyday Purification

At most Shinto shrines (*jinja*), the *chozuya* or *temizuya*—those stone basins fed by running water—serve as a threshold ritual. The sequence is deliberate and symbolic:

1. **Rinse the left hand** first (representing the "receiving" side).

2. **Rinse the right hand** next.

3. **Pour water into the left palm** and rinse your mouth (spit it out—never swallow).

4. **Rinse the left hand** once more to cleanse any lingering impurity.

This isn't just hygiene; it's a mindful reset, washing away the dust of the mundane world (*yo-na-iro*) to approach the *kami* (spirits or deities) with clarity. Fun fact: During the Edo period (1603–1868), this practice spread widely, influenced by tea ceremony (*chanoyu*) traditions that also emphasize water's cleansing power.

### Misogi: The Immersive Ascetic Practice

For deeper catharsis, *misogi* takes it to elemental extremes. Rooted in ancient *yamabushi* mountain asceticism, it often means enduring a frigid waterfall (*taki-gyo*)—think icy torrents in places like Nachi Falls in Wakayama Prefecture, one of Japan's most sacred sites. Practitioners chant *norito* prayers while the water pounds them, symbolizing the shedding of ego and sins. It's not for the faint-hearted; hypothermia is a real risk, but participants describe it as euphoric, akin to a spiritual rebirth.

Historically, misogi ties back to myths like the *Kojiki* (712 CE), where deities like Izanagi purify themselves in rivers after chaotic underworld journeys. Today, it's practiced by Shinto priests, martial artists (e.g., in aikido), and even tourists seeking that Instagram-worthy enlightenment.
