# Māyā (माया) - ⫻

Created at 2025/09/08 6:35 PM

**Māyā** (माया):

- **Definition**: Māyā means "illusion," "magic," or "creative power." In Indian philosophy, it has multiple nuances:
    - **Advaita Vedanta**: Māyā is the illusory power that veils the true nature of reality (*Brahman*), causing the individual to perceive the world as separate from the self (*Ātman*). It is the force that creates the appearance of multiplicity and duality, binding beings to *saṃsāra* (the cycle of birth and death).
    - **Tantric/Shakta Traditions**: Māyā is the creative energy of the divine (often associated with *Shakti*), manifesting the universe as an expression of divine consciousness. It is not merely illusion but the divine power of creation and transformation.
    - **General Context**: Māyā can represent the deceptive nature of the material world, where appearances obscure the underlying unity of existence, or the creative force that shapes reality.
- **Symbolic Role**: Māyā is often depicted as a web or veil that ensnares beings in illusion but can also be transcended through discriminative knowledge (*viveka*) or spiritual practice to realize the truth.

**The mind cannot hold “absolute, non-dual consciousness” and “finite, differentiated experience” in the same cognitive frame.**

Everything else—veiling, projecting, creating, seducing, binding, liberating—is a secondary narrative spun to account for the primary cognitive fracture.

Break it down:

1. **Non-dual ground** (Brahman, Śiva, pure Awareness) is without edge, object or sequence.
2. **A sentient organism** nevertheless must navigate a world of edges, objects and sequences to survive.
3. **The survival interface** (manas, ahaṃkāra, antaḥ-karaṇa, brain, language, culture) therefore:

    - slices the field into “this/not-this,”
    - tags each slice with “I/not-I,”
    - strings the slices into time.

4. **That slicing activity is experienced as “the world.”** Because the organism forgets it is its own cut, the world appears as an external, independent reality.
5. **Any philosophy then calls this self-forgetting cut** māyā, śakti, avidyā, mātrā, śūnyatā, etc.

Hence the converging root cause is not a “thing” but a structural blind-spot built into embodied cognition: **the inability to notice the act of differentiation while the act is occurring.**

All the secondary questions—why the power exists, whether it is good or bad, how to undo it—are simply different cultural answers to the same core discovery: **when awareness misses its own role as projector, the projected world is taken as final truth, and bondage follows.**
